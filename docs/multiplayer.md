# Multiplayer architecture

The playable network slice uses invite-based rooms. `src/network/peerRoom.ts` opens a Trystero room using a 10-character random room code. Trystero's default public Nostr relays exchange connection information; gameplay packets travel between browsers over WebRTC data channels. GitHub Pages serves only static files. There is no project-operated room server or per-session map request.

Each browser renders the arena locally. `src/game/playerController.ts` supplies its local pose at up to 20 updates per second. The versioned `pose-v1` action includes an arena key, sequence number, location, view, jump height, and movement flags, including `sliding`, `airborne` and `lean`. Protocol 5 added spare magazines, dropped guns, knife strokes, open doors and hatches and venting gas cylinders. Protocol 6 added the market's doors, steam pipes and record shop. Protocol 7 added fuel drums, extinguishers and the piles broken stalls leave. Protocol 8 added smoke grenades (a `smoke` flag on the throw intent and event, a `smoke` event when one pops, and a smoke count per player in snapshot version 6). Protocol 9 moved positions to a lane of their own, protocol 10 packs them into 25 bytes, and protocol 11 has the coordinator send only what has changed; none mixes with earlier builds.

Positions travel as 25 bytes (protocol, sequence, send time, east, north and jump height in centimetres, heading, pitch and lean in hundredths, five movement flags in one byte) on a second WebRTC data channel on each connection, beside Trystero's, which both peers open as `positions` with the same id (negotiated, so it needs no signalling): unordered and with no retransmission. A lost position holds up nothing behind it, and a congested lane skips a position rather than queue it. Everything else, which must arrive, stays on Trystero's reliable, ordered channel. A position is rejected if it lies further from the last accepted one than the player could have run in the time between them by their own clock; after 1.5 s without a position the next is taken as it is, so a stalled connection no longer freezes a player for good. See [network benchmarks](network-benchmarks.md) for measurements. Receivers reject out-of-order, malformed, wrong-arena, out-of-bounds, or implausibly large position changes. An accepted respawn resets the position check. `src/game/remotePlayers.ts` draws each remote player 100 ms behind its newest pose, interpolating between packets placed on the sender's own clock (the smallest observed receive-minus-send offset, relaxed slowly to follow drift), with up to 100 ms of extrapolation and no gliding across teleports. Each peer computes a spawn slot from the sorted connected peer IDs so newly joined players do not occupy the same point. The room link includes the current arena URL parameters.

`src/game/combatRules.ts` defines the knife, pistol and carbine, the knife's strokes, the guns' magazines and what everything weighs, deterministic pickup positions, weapon range and damage. Rounds travel through the physics world surface by surface, penetrating by material and damaging what they cross ([physics notes](physics.md)). `src/game/matchState.ts` owns health, inventory, the rounds in each gun and each spare magazine, reloads that swap magazines, kills, deaths, respawns, dropped guns, and pickup returns (30 seconds for guns, 25 for magazines). It ignores stale player poses for attacks and pickups. The lowest connected peer ID temporarily coordinates combat. Other peers send attack and pickup intents; the coordinator checks range, cover, ammo, cooldown, and proximity, and applies movement spread and bloom to each shot ([movement notes](movement.md)), before broadcasting a versioned combat snapshot. The coordinator keeps the 32 most recent events, so one shot can report every body and object it crossed and a grenade can report everyone it hurt. A peer that joins, or asks to catch up, gets the whole snapshot with those events and starts from the newest. Broadcasts go out at most 20 times a second. A new coordinator's first one carries the whole snapshot; each after it carries only what has changed since the one before: the fields of each player that changed (a shot sends that gun's rounds, not the whole player), the world objects whose health changed, whichever of the pickups, grenades, drops and doors changed, and the events since. The channel is reliable and ordered, so none go missing, and before sending a peer the whole snapshot the coordinator sends out pending changes, so later changes build on exactly what that peer holds. A peer applies changes only from the coordinator whose state it holds and only on top of the revision they were made from; changes it cannot place make it ask for the whole snapshot again, at most every two seconds. The first whole snapshot from a new coordinator replaces what a peer holds whatever its revision. Numbers in updates go to four decimal places, a tenth of a millimetre in a position, which made a shot event a third smaller. Peers play every event newer than the last they saw. They also carry the health of damaged world objects, live grenades and dropped guns with where they fell from and came to rest, so a new coordinator can finish a grenade already in the air and every peer draws a drop tumbling along the same path. Peers retain the latest snapshot so another can take over when the coordinator leaves.

**Bandwidth.** Snapshots used to resend all 32 recent events every time, which made nine-tenths of each packet, and every accepted shot sent one. In a three-player room, all firing carbines on one machine, the coordinator sent 13 KB snapshots ten times a second to each peer, about 125 KB/s. On real connections that much reliable, ordered traffic queues up behind any lost packet, and the poses that share the channel stall with it, so players stutter and shots land late. Now the same fight sends 2 KB snapshots twelve times a second, 21 KB/s, and every peer played every event, 221 in 15 s, with no gaps. Handling a snapshot takes under 2 ms. If the coordinator vanishes without leaving, as when its browser crashes, the others take about 11 s to notice and hand over, then play carries on.

Lag compensation: each attack carries, for every player on the shooter's screen, the moment of that player's own pose timeline being drawn. The coordinator keeps 3.5 s of pose history per player on the same clock and rewinds each target to that moment, by at most 350 ms, before testing hit boxes. A rewound target is hit where the shooter saw it; claims older than the limit are clamped rather than trusted. Grenade throws and falls are intents too; a fall is accepted only as far as the drop shows in the player's own recent poses. The coordinator also simulates loose props and sends their transforms on a separate `props-v1` action; other peers send the shoves their players give props on `prop-push-v1`, capped at what a body can push. Knife strokes arrive as attacks naming the stroke when the blade lands, rather than when the button is pressed. `src/game/weaponVisuals.ts` renders held weapons and street pickups.

This is a prototype authority model. Positions still come from clients, so a modified client could move illegally despite the distance and bounds checks. Full input-based movement validation and correction need a stronger authority protocol, and lag compensation trusts each shooter's report of what it was drawing within the rewind limit. The coordinator is chosen deterministically but has no heartbeat or epoch-based handoff yet; contested joins and abrupt disconnects need more testing. A connection may also need a TURN relay when direct WebRTC cannot cross a particular network; the public signaling relays do not supply that relay. Their availability is outside this project's control, so production matchmaking needs a monitored signaling and TURN plan.

### Combat feedback and hit zones

Attack intents carry the aim angles captured when the trigger is pressed, or when a knife stroke lands; the leader uses its most recent accepted position and stance. The leader resolves oriented head, torso and leg volumes, static Rapier obstruction, range, cooldown, reload, ammo and health. Headshots multiply damage by 2.5, leg hits by 0.7; firearms lose up to 45% damage toward maximum range. These are gameplay tuning values, not wound simulation. Confirmed damage is capped at remaining health.

Every accepted attack emits a replicated event, including misses, with its ray and stopping distance. Clients display a short tracer, impact puff, confirmed damage and elimination feedback. Effects are bounded to 48 active groups and share geometry. Holding fire in pointer lock repeats the carbine; pistol fire remains semi-automatic. Sprinting prevents attacks. Camera recoil changes subsequent aim.

Shots remain hitscan: the trail is feedback, not a simulated bullet with travel time or drop. Players use approximate anatomical volumes rather than per-bone collision; remote interpolation, network latency and leader trust still limit competitive accuracy. Ammunition is finite: a gun comes with its one magazine, and spares are found or taken from fallen players.

The Trystero room name is the room code with `@night-market` appended, and every packet carries the key `night-market-v1`, which peers check before accepting it.
