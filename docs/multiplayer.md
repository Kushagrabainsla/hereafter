# Multiplayer architecture

The playable network slice uses invite-based rooms. `src/network/peerRoom.ts` opens a Trystero room using a 10-character random room code. Seven public Nostr relays, listed in `peerRoom.ts`, exchange connection information; gameplay packets travel between browsers over WebRTC data channels. GitHub Pages serves only static files. There is no project-operated room server or per-session map request.

Each browser renders the arena locally. `src/game/playerController.ts` supplies its local pose at up to 30 updates per second. The versioned `pose-v1` action includes an arena key, sequence number, location, view, jump height, and movement flags, including `sliding`, `airborne` and `lean`. Protocol 5 added spare magazines, dropped guns, knife strokes, open doors and hatches and venting gas cylinders. Protocol 6 added the market's doors, steam pipes and record shop. Protocol 7 added fuel drums, extinguishers and the piles broken stalls leave. Protocol 8 added smoke grenades (a `smoke` flag on the throw intent and event, a `smoke` event when one pops, and a smoke count per player in snapshot version 6). Protocol 9 moved positions to a lane of their own, protocol 10 packs them into 25 bytes, protocol 11 has the coordinator send only what has changed, protocol 12 chooses the coordinator by how quickly it can answer, protocol 13 replaces a coordinator that goes silent or whose tab goes to the background, protocol 14 lets a player connect while the world loads, and protocol 15 sends intents again to a new coordinator, with the number of each player's last handled intent in snapshot version 7; none mixes with earlier builds.

Positions travel as 25 bytes (protocol, sequence, send time, east, north and jump height in centimetres, heading, pitch and lean in hundredths, and one byte of flags: five for movement and one saying the player's tab is in the background) on a second WebRTC data channel on each connection, beside Trystero's, which both peers open as `positions` with the same id (negotiated, so it needs no signalling): unordered and with no retransmission. A lost position holds up nothing behind it, and a congested lane skips a position rather than queue it. A player whose position has not changed sends it only every quarter second instead of every 33 ms, which keeps the coordinator, which ignores positions over 1.5 s old, counting them present. When they move again, the position they held goes out first, stamped at the moment before, so other players and the coordinator's hit checks see them set off then, not glide from where the last position put them. Everything else, which must arrive, stays on Trystero's reliable, ordered channel. A position is rejected if it lies further from the last accepted one than the player could have run in the time between them by their own clock; after 1.5 s without a position the next is taken as it is, so a stalled connection no longer freezes a player for good. See [network benchmarks](network-benchmarks.md) for measurements. Receivers reject out-of-order, malformed, wrong-arena, out-of-bounds, or implausibly large position changes. An accepted respawn resets the position check. `src/game/remotePlayers.ts` draws each remote player just far enough behind its newest pose that the next has almost always arrived: the time between its poses while it moves plus how late nine in ten of its last 64 arrived beyond the quickest, between 40 and 150 ms, changing at a tenth of real time so motion never jumps. It used to be a fixed 100 ms. It interpolates between packets placed on the sender's own clock (the smallest observed receive-minus-send offset, relaxed slowly to follow drift), with up to 100 ms of extrapolation and no gliding across teleports. Each peer computes a spawn slot from the sorted connected peer IDs so newly joined players do not occupy the same point. The room link includes the current arena URL parameters.

`src/game/combatRules.ts` defines the knife, pistol and carbine, the knife's strokes, the guns' magazines and what everything weighs, deterministic pickup positions, weapon range and damage. Rounds travel through the physics world surface by surface, penetrating by material and damaging what they cross ([physics notes](physics.md)). `src/game/matchState.ts` owns health, inventory, the rounds in each gun and each spare magazine, reloads that swap magazines, kills, deaths, respawns, dropped guns, and pickup returns (30 seconds for guns, 25 for magazines). It ignores stale player poses for attacks and pickups. One peer coordinates combat; how it is chosen is described below. Other peers send attack and pickup intents; the coordinator checks range, cover, ammo, cooldown, and proximity, and applies movement spread and bloom to each shot ([movement notes](movement.md)), before broadcasting a versioned combat snapshot. The coordinator keeps the 32 most recent events, so one shot can report every body and object it crossed and a grenade can report everyone it hurt. A peer that joins, or asks to catch up, gets the whole snapshot with those events and starts from the newest. Broadcasts go out at most 20 times a second. A new coordinator's first one carries the whole snapshot; each after it carries only what has changed since the one before: the fields of each player that changed (a shot sends that gun's rounds, not the whole player), the world objects whose health changed, whichever of the pickups, grenades, drops and doors changed, and the events since. The channel is reliable and ordered, so none go missing, and before sending a peer the whole snapshot the coordinator sends out pending changes, so later changes build on exactly what that peer holds. A peer applies changes only from the coordinator whose state it holds and only on top of the revision they were made from; changes it cannot place make it ask for the whole snapshot again, at most every two seconds. The first whole snapshot from a new coordinator replaces what a peer holds whatever its revision. Numbers in updates go to four decimal places, a tenth of a millimetre in a position, which made a shot event a third smaller. Peers play every event newer than the last they saw. They also carry the health of damaged world objects, live grenades and dropped guns with where they fell from and came to rest, so a new coordinator can finish a grenade already in the air and every peer draws a drop tumbling along the same path. Peers retain the latest snapshot so another can take over when the coordinator leaves.

**Bandwidth.** Snapshots used to resend all 32 recent events every time, which made nine-tenths of each packet, and every accepted shot sent one. In a three-player room, all firing carbines on one machine, the coordinator sent 13 KB snapshots ten times a second to each peer, about 125 KB/s. On real connections that much reliable, ordered traffic queues up behind any lost packet, and the poses that share the channel stall with it, so players stutter and shots land late. Now the same fight sends 2 KB snapshots twelve times a second, 21 KB/s, and every peer played every event, 221 in 15 s, with no gaps. Handling a snapshot takes under 2 ms.

**Joining.** An invite link joins its room as the page opens, so the connection is made while the world loads rather than after it: three trips through the signalling relays and the WebRTC handshake. A player still loading says so when it connects. It takes no part in the match, and no one chooses it as coordinator, until its world is ready; then it tells every peer it is in and asks the coordinator for the match. A friend opening a link for the first time, on a 25 Mbit/s connection, used to start playing 2.1 to 2.2 s after the world loaded; now 0.34 to 0.41 s. A loaded world draws its first frame only after building every shader, which on a busy machine holds the page still for many seconds; a connection waiting on the page waits that long too.

**Choosing the coordinator.** A new room settles on its lowest peer ID among the players in the game. Every peer tells each newcomer whom it follows, with an epoch: a lone peer is at epoch 0, a coordinator moves to epoch 1 once every peer in its new room has answered, and each handover adds one. A peer follows an announced coordinator whose epoch is higher, or the same with a lower ID, once connected to it, so a player who joins later never displaces the coordinator, whatever its ID; before this, a newcomer with a lower ID took over and the match changed hands mid-fight. A peer that has heard of a settled room never settles one of its own while it waits to reach that room's coordinator. Every two seconds each peer measures its round trip to every other peer, from its connections' WebRTC statistics, and how late a timer due every 100 ms runs, which is how long its main thread keeps an arriving message waiting, and reports the round trips and the median of its last ten two-second medians to the coordinator. The coordinator also repeats whom the room follows, for any peer that missed it. It estimates what each peer would add to every shot as coordinator, its average round trip to the others plus that wait, and hands over to the best peer when the typical one, the median, would add at most 60% as much and at least 8 ms less, three checks running, and not within 30 s of taking over: it sends out its last changes, announces the new coordinator at the next epoch, and follows it. Each of those rules came from a measurement. Frame rate is no guide: a processor slowed to a quarter speed still drew as many frames, the graphics card being the limit, while its timers ran four times as late. Comparing the coordinator with the best of five busy peers, rather than the typical one, handed over by chance. And the coordinator itself stalls for a few seconds in a fight, when every shot comes to it at once or a blast throws props it alone simulates, and recovers once that passes; a twenty-second median ignores such stalls, while a slow machine, slow throughout, still hands over within about fifteen seconds. A coordinator that leaves is succeeded by the lowest ID left, at the same epoch, which hands over in turn if another peer would do better. Upload is not weighed: since the coordinator sends only changes, six players need about 40 KB/s from it, which any home connection has.

**Losing the coordinator.** A coordinator that leaves the page says so, and the others carry on within a few milliseconds. One that vanishes, because its tab crashed or was closed, its connection dropped or its computer slept, used to leave everyone without shots for 12 to 13 s: Trystero waits for the connection itself to fail, which the browser reports after 5 to 7.5 s, then waits 5 s more. Closing a tab was no better: Trystero's goodbye goes out only once its message is ready, and a closing tab is gone by then, so the others waited 12 to 14 s for the connection to fail. Now a leaving page, on `pagehide` as well as `beforeunload`, sends a single byte, the protocol number, on each position lane, which the others take as goodbye, and closes its connections at once. A closing tab also goes to the background first, so a coordinator hands over before it goes. For the rest, every peer notes when it last heard from each other peer. Each sends its position at least four times a second, so a coordinator unheard for 2 s has gone: the peer that notices names the lowest ID it still hears, preferring players whose tabs are in front, as coordinator at the next epoch, and the others follow the announcement. A peer that hears none of the others has lost its own connection and does nothing. A peer whose own page was busy reads the messages that arrived meanwhile only after its check, so it acts only if the coordinator is still unheard at its next check, a quarter of a second later. In six-player fights on one overloaded machine, a coordinator froze for at most 0.8 s, and players went up to 2 s without hearing it only while frozen themselves. A coordinator stalled rather than gone hears the higher epoch when it recovers and follows. A coordinator whose tab goes to the background, where the browser runs its timers once a second and draws no frames, so its props stop and its updates wait, hands over at once to a player whose tab is in front; each position carries that flag. Before, it held on for 14 s, until its slowness showed in its reports.

Lag compensation: each attack carries, for every player on the shooter's screen, the moment of that player's own pose timeline being drawn. The coordinator keeps 3.5 s of pose history per player on the same clock and rewinds each target to that moment, by at most 350 ms, before testing hit boxes. A rewound target is hit where the shooter saw it; claims older than the limit are clamped rather than trusted. Grenade throws and falls are intents too; a fall is accepted only as far as the drop shows in the player's own recent poses. The coordinator also simulates loose props and sends their transforms on a separate `props-v1` action; other peers send the shoves their players give props on `prop-push-v1`, capped at what a body can push. Knife strokes arrive as attacks naming the stroke when the blade lands, rather than when the button is pressed. `src/game/weaponVisuals.ts` renders held weapons and street pickups.

This is a prototype authority model. Positions still come from clients, so a modified client could move illegally despite the distance and bounds checks. Full input-based movement validation and correction need a stronger authority protocol, and lag compensation trusts each shooter's report of what it was drawing within the rewind limit. Handovers are epoch-based. An intent that reaches the old coordinator just after it has handed over, or never reaches one that has gone, would be dropped, so each player sends again to a new coordinator its intents from the last 300 ms that the state it holds does not show handled; the state records the number of the last intent handled from each player, so a new coordinator never handles one twice. Intents older than that are not resent: a coordinator that has gone is noticed only after 2 s, and a shot resolved seconds after it was fired would hit the wrong place. A connection may also need a TURN relay when direct WebRTC cannot cross a particular network; the public signaling relays do not supply that relay. Their availability is outside this project's control: Trystero's own pick of five included two that had stopped passing messages on, so the game names seven that passed every message in three rounds of tests, and the list needs checking from time to time. Production matchmaking needs a monitored signaling and TURN plan.

### Combat feedback and hit zones

Attack intents carry the aim angles captured when the trigger is pressed, or when a knife stroke lands; the leader uses its most recent accepted position and stance. The leader resolves oriented head, torso and leg volumes, static Rapier obstruction, range, cooldown, reload, ammo and health. Headshots multiply damage by 2.5, leg hits by 0.7; firearms lose up to 45% damage toward maximum range. These are gameplay tuning values, not wound simulation. Confirmed damage is capped at remaining health.

Every accepted attack emits a replicated event, including misses, with its ray and stopping distance. Clients display a short tracer, impact puff, confirmed damage and elimination feedback. Effects are bounded to 48 active groups and share geometry. Holding fire in pointer lock repeats the carbine; pistol fire remains semi-automatic. Sprinting prevents attacks. Camera recoil changes subsequent aim.

Shots remain hitscan: the trail is feedback, not a simulated bullet with travel time or drop. Players use approximate anatomical volumes rather than per-bone collision; remote interpolation, network latency and leader trust still limit competitive accuracy. Ammunition is finite: a gun comes with its one magazine, and spares are found or taken from fallen players.

The Trystero room name is the room code with `@night-market` appended, and every packet carries the key `night-market-v1`, which peers check before accepting it.
