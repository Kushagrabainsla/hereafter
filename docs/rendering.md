# Rendering

## The Night Market's look

**Sky.** `src/world/duskSky.ts` paints a 2048 × 1024 equirectangular dusk sky at load: a deep blue zenith fading through violet and rose to an orange band where the sun has just gone, a warm glow toward the sun's bearing, low clouds lit from below, faint city haze over the rooftops, 1,800 stars where the sky has darkened, and dithering against banding. It is both the background and the environment every surface reflects. A low orange sun, about 15° up, catches the upper floors and casts the only shadows; the alleys below are lit by the market itself. A cool hemisphere light and a violet haze fill the shadows.

**Lights.** On WebGPU, Three.js `ClusteredLighting` (Forward+ clustered shading) sorts lights into screen tiles and depth slices with a compute pass (a fixed 40 × 24 grid of tiles and 16 slices, so the grid never changes with the screen; see Resolution below), so each pixel shades only the lights that reach it and the shader does not grow with the light count. The market has 90 real point lights: three along each bulb string, one under each stall awning, two per lantern row, every wall lamp, a spill of light outside each lit shop, one beside each neon sign, and the lights of the restaurant, the bar, the record shop and the food truck. WebGL 2 cannot run the clustering pass, so it keeps six key lights and draws soft pools of light on the ground instead. Lamps can be shot out (`src/world/lamps.ts`): a broken lamp's glow moves to a dark copy or its sign's emission drops, and each real light shines in proportion to the lamps still feeding it. On WebGL each key light follows the lamps within 7 m of it. The first-person lighting reads the same intensities, so hands go dark in a darkened alley too. Without clustering, an earlier test with 31 lights took more than nine seconds to build its shaders.

**Baked light.** Real-time lights shine straight onto surfaces, but light that bounces, and the way corners, the ground under awnings and the foot of narrow alleys see less of the sky, has to be computed offline. `npm run build:lightmaps` (`scripts/lightmaps/`) does it from the map's own code. A tool page builds the market in headless Chrome, flattens every static surface into a 2048 px lightmap atlas at about 15 texels per metre, and leaves out faces no player can see (rooftops, undersides, faces buried in or flush against other solids, and outer walls facing away from the market). Blender's Cycles then bakes two maps on the GPU, in about ten minutes on an Apple M4: sky visibility (ambient occlusion within 6 m) and light from the 90 lamps and the low sun after at least one bounce, with the stalls, the food truck and the rest of the kit (fire escapes, air conditioners, the record shop's fittings) as occluders. Doors move, so they are left out. Each chart is smoothed on its own, and the bounce map is scaled and sRGB-encoded. The 0.5 MB of WebP images and the atlas coordinates (`public/lightmaps/`) load with the textures. `src/world/lightmap.ts` gives the static surfaces those coordinates. Sky visibility then occludes their sky and environment light, so rooms lose the cold sky fill on their walls and wet paving stops mirroring the violet sky inside covered spots. The bounce is added in proportion to each surface's colour, lifted 1.6 times to stand in for light from signs, lit windows and lanterns, which the bake leaves out. A checksum of each surface's geometry is stored with the bake, so a map changed without rebaking keeps real-time light only and warns in the console.

**Glow and grading.** The world renders once into a half-float target and the first-person view into its own multisampled (4×) pass, in a `RenderPipeline`. Ground-truth ambient occlusion (GTAO) runs at half resolution from the world's depth, rebuilding normals from it rather than writing a normal buffer, and darkens contact and creases the lightmap is too coarse for, and anything that moves. SMAA then smooths the world's edges, since the effect needs a plain depth buffer that multisampling would not give it. SMAA finds edges by differences in colour, and in linear light the night market is so dark that a dim door frame against a dark wall differed by less than its threshold, so most edges went unsmoothed; it now sees the world at the game's exposure and gamma-encoded, as the eye does, and its result is decoded back. At a 16-inch MacBook's size that turned stepped diagonals such as the table braces into smooth lines and took about 4% of the hard steps out of the frame. The view is laid over the world by its own coverage, since both clear to transparent; drawing straight to the canvas after the pipeline would replace its output. Bloom (strength 0.75, radius 0.55, threshold 0.9) makes bulbs, lanterns, neon tubes, lit windows and muzzle flashes glow. A grade cools the shadows, warms the highlights and adds a touch of saturation, a soft vignette darkens the corners, ACES tone mapping and the sRGB conversion follow, and fine film grain over the result breaks up banding and the too-clean look of rendered surfaces. Screen-space reflections were tried for the wet paving: with the normal and roughness buffers they need, they cut the frame rate at Retina resolution to a third, so the paving keeps reflecting the sky and the real-time lights instead. On WebGPU, one more clustered light flares at each muzzle and each grenade burst, so shots and blasts light the alley for a moment.

**Surfaces.** Walls use node materials that darken toward the ground over the first three metres and in broad, noisy patches, so facades look weathered rather than evenly tiled. Paving and asphalt carry puddles from world-space fractal noise: inside a puddle the surface turns dark and nearly mirror-smooth, so it picks up the lights and the sky. Every window is a modelled unit set on the wall: a painted frame and casing, a dark reveal, sash bars, a stone sill on corbels and a lintel with a keystone; its glass is a card in front of the reveal. A cornice runs along the top of each facade and a string course under its windows, and roller shutters close the passages at the alleys' ends. Unlit panes are dark glass reflecting the dusk, and lit ones show one of eight painted rooms: curtains, blinds, a television's blue light, a plant, a kitchen, a lamp, a half curtain or a pink-lit room. Neon signs are glass tubes along the letters and a border on a dark backer, so only the tubes glow; shop, kitchen and banner signs are lit boxes with raised letters. Six vertical blade signs hang over the alleys. Paper posters, taped up and peeling at a corner, and a sprayed mural cover parts of the service alley. Downpipes, air conditioners, condensers, meters, vents, back doors, fire escapes and the stalls with their goods come from a modelled kit (`src/world/kit.ts`), one instanced mesh per piece and kept-apart material: every plain material of a piece is merged into one mesh whose vertices carry each part's colour, roughness and metalness, so a piece of many materials draws in one call ([asset pipeline](asset-pipeline.md)). The kit and the pipeline's families are compressed with meshopt and unpacked on load. The lanterns, bulbs, lamps and signs are modelled pieces from the pipeline too; neon signs are glass tubes bent into the letters, lit by the game. Each stall's torn awning and collapsed pile are instances too, kept at zero scale until they are needed. The grill stalls' coals glow, so bloom picks them up. Only the fire escapes cast shadows, since only they stand high enough in the low sun. Smoke, steam and powder are soft particles in a scene of their own (`steamPipes.ts`). Each billow reads the world pass's depth at its screen position and fades over its last 0.9 m in front of what is behind it, which also hides it behind walls without a depth test. The cloud pass is laid over the world after ambient occlusion and SMAA, by its own coverage like the hands. Drawn in the world pass, the occlusion would trace the creases of the walls behind a cloud in dark lines across it. Billows around the eye cannot cover the view the way a real cloud does, so the viewer also measures how thick the clouds are at the eye and lays up to 90% grey haze over the world, under the hands. three's own soft-particle helper names a GLSL reserved word, so the fade is written out for WebGL to compile.

**Detail and motion.** Cedar water tanks on steel stands and TV antennas break the roofline against the sky, cables sag across both alleys, and the food truck is a full model (see [the Night Market](night-market.md#the-food-truck)) with weathered paint that darkens toward the road. Lanterns sway, the bar's OPEN sign stutters, and steam rises from the food truck's roof fan, a stall and a manhole.

**Cost.** Measured in headless Chrome on an Apple M4 at the spawn, with about 430 draw calls and 263,000 triangles. The baked light and post-processing were measured while the machine was heavily loaded (load average 20 to 40), against the previous release at the same time:

| Resolution | Previous release | Now |
|---|---|---|
| 1440 × 813 | 60 FPS (the frame cap) | 60 FPS |
| 2880 × 1626, a Retina screen's pixel count | 32 FPS | 24 to 27 FPS |
| 1440 × 813 on WebGL 2 (`?renderer=webgl`) | 34 FPS | 40 FPS (no multisampling of the world) |

On an idle machine the previous release held 60 FPS at Retina resolution and 33 to 39 at 4K; GTAO, SMAA and grain together cost about a fifth of the frame there. **Resolution.** The world renders at most 4.2 million pixels, up to twice the CSS resolution. A 16-inch MacBook Pro's Retina screen gets 1.48×, a 13-inch Air 1.73× and a 5K display 1.07×. Changing the drawing size used to rebuild every lit shader, because three's clustered lighting writes its grid of screen tiles into each one. On a 16-inch Retina screen each rebuild froze the page for up to 13 s. The old renderer also lowered its resolution a quarter step at a time whenever the frame rate dipped, so a large screen froze again and again just after loading. An earlier note blamed headless emulation for the 2 FPS this gave at a 2× device scale; that was this freeze. Now `src/world/fixedClusters.ts` keeps the grid at 40 × 24 tiles at every size and finds each fragment's tile through a uniform, so no resize rebuilds a shader. The size is chosen when the window settles, 0.3 s after the last resize. After four seconds under 40 FPS the budget drops to 55%, at most twice, and never climbs back, but never below the CSS resolution: on a 16-inch MacBook the second drop used to reach 0.81×, where every edge turned to blocks of pixels, and now stops at 1×. If the graphics device is lost, the loading card returns with the error instead of a frozen frame.

| Emulated screen (CSS size at 2×) | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| 13-inch Air, 1470 × 956 | 4 to 14 FPS, frames frozen up to 9.4 s | 48 FPS, worst frame 50 ms |
| 16-inch Pro, 1728 × 1117 | 2 to 4 FPS, up to 12.9 s | 48 FPS, worst 34 ms |
| 5K display, 2560 × 1440 | 1 to 1.5 FPS, up to 23 s | 49 FPS, worst 33 ms |
| Dragging the window wider, then fullscreen | 2.5 s freeze per resize, after coarser tiles | worst frame 50 ms |

**Many players.** With eleven remote players in view, the main thread's time goes almost entirely to three preparing and submitting draws, not to the GPU or the network. On a CPU slowed threefold, a crowded room dropped from 57 to 34 FPS. About 690 objects passed through the renderer each frame, 300 of them through its full per-object update. Half of those came from redrawing the sun's shadow map. Two changes cut this:

- **The sun's shadow map is drawn once.** The low sun only reaches the upper floors, where nothing moves, so its shadow covers the whole map from a fixed place (`shadow.autoUpdate` off). It used to follow the camera and be redrawn every frame, with every remote player in it.
- **Remote players out of view are neither drawn nor animated.** three never culls the characters, because their skinned bounds are unreliable, so `RemotePlayers.update` tests each one against the view itself. Players beyond 18 m animate every second frame and beyond 35 m every third, with the skipped time caught up.

WebGPU render bundles (`BundleGroup`) were tried for the static scenery and made it slower: three r186 still runs its full update for every bundled object each frame, so a bundle saves only the GPU command encoding.

| Renderer CPU per frame, median of three alternating runs | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Alone | 13.7 ms | 10.0 ms |
| Eleven players running in view | 21.0 ms | 16.3 ms |
| Eleven players firing carbines in view | 20.3 ms | 17.4 ms |
| Eleven players firing behind the camera | 6.6 ms | 4.2 ms |

These runs shared the machine with other applications, so single runs varied up to threefold; the new build was faster in ten of twelve paired runs. Most of the remaining cost with everyone firing is the effects of each shot.

## Approach

Each player renders the same bundled map locally and sends pose and combat data over WebRTC; peers do not stream geometry or animation bones. [Rapier 3D](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/getting_started_js/) runs character physics and scene queries in WebAssembly. Before claiming 2–8 player performance, test several real MacBooks and Windows GPUs in a live room, record frame-time percentiles and memory use, and inspect worst-case views with other players and weapon effects visible.

Modern FPS weapon handling benefits from responsive camera and view-model motion: turn settling, stance transitions, sway, and near-wall behaviour are described in [Call of Duty's movement notes](https://www.callofduty.com/blog/2026/08/call-of-duty-modern-warfare-4-next-highlights-multiplayer-gameplay-systems). The first-person arms and the other players come from a licensed, rigged character asset with blended clips (below). [Three.js AnimationMixer](https://threejs.org/manual/pages/animation-system.html) crossfades the locomotion clips.

### Authored first-person assets

`public/models/knife.glb`, `pistol.glb` and `rifle.glb` are the bundled first-person view models: the arms, gloves and wristwatch of the Microsoft Rocketbox avatar `Military_Male_03` (MIT) holding the Poly Haven [Fish Knife](https://polyhaven.com/a/fish_knife) and [Service Pistol](https://polyhaven.com/a/service_pistol) (CC0) and the OpenGameArt [AKM](https://opengameart.org/content/akm) by LonesomeDucky (CC0). Sources, authors and licences are recorded in [data sources](data-sources.md). The files are 1.5–1.8 MB each with WebP textures: the arms share one repacked 1200 px atlas that keeps the source's own detail (each arm's island is about 600 × 1100 px in the 2K body atlas), and the AKM's 4K maps are reduced to 1K. KTX2/Basis would cut their GPU memory further but needs the Basis transcoder shipped with the site, so it is left for the general texture pass.

`AuthoredViewModel` (`src/game/authoredViewModel.ts`) loads the same-origin GLB named by `VITE_KNIFE_VIEWMODEL`, `VITE_PISTOL_VIEWMODEL` and `VITE_RIFLE_VIEWMODEL` (set in the committed `.env`, so dev, `build` and `build:pages` all bundle them). Each file is authored in camera-local metres (+Y up, forward -Z) and attached directly to the first-person camera without rescaling. Clips are named `idle`, `walk`, `run` and `fire`, and the knife has `slash` and `stab` instead of `fire`. Locomotion clips crossfade over 150 ms. Attack clips start on the neutral pose and are converted to additive clips at load, so recoil, the knife strokes and the muzzle flash layer over whichever locomotion clip is playing. The knife strokes are authored 2.5 times slower than real time so their fast arcs get enough keyframes, and play 2.5 times faster; the loader clones it first because exporters share one accessor between identical constant tracks and the additive conversion subtracts in place. A node named `muzzle` carries the flash geometry (scaled to nothing outside the fire clip) and gives the tracer its start point. The spring sway and breathing ([movement notes](movement.md#how-the-held-weapon-moves)) and the lower-and-raise weapon switch come from the procedural `WeaponVisuals` state (`dynamics()`). A knife stroke all but stops for 75 ms when it bites into a body, and a blade turned by something hard fades out over 160 ms while the hand jolts back on a critically damped spring. A model that is missing or fails to load is not drawn: there are no stand-in hands or guns.

The files hold each gun yawed 13 to 15° toward the screen centre and canted slightly. Its barrel therefore crossed the line of sight at one range and pointed off to the side everywhere else, while tracers left the muzzle for the crosshair, so the gun looked tilted against its own shots. At load, `straighten` turns the whole arms-and-weapon rig about the grip by the inverse of the weapon's rest rotation in the idle clip. The barrel then runs parallel to the view axis. Lines parallel to the view axis meet at the screen centre, so the barrel points at the crosshair from every range and the tracer continues it. In headless Chrome, both guns' barrel lines met the screen within 2 px of the crosshair once raised, and the idle sway moved them by less than half a degree. The knife keeps its authored angle.

`src/game/weaponModels.ts` loads each file once. The first-person view uses the whole file. Pickups and other players use a copy of just its `weapon` node without the muzzle flash, sharing geometry and textures, so every weapon in the game is the same model. A pickup spins the model about its centre, drawn 1.6 times life size for the pistol and knife and 1.05 times for the carbine so it reads at a distance. Until the files load, a pickup shows only its stand and characters hold nothing.

### Making the hands look real

The source avatar's hands are a game asset of about 1,200 triangles an arm, and the first version lit them with a fixed studio light, so they looked like grey plastic in front of a warm, lamp-lit alley. Three things changed.

**Shape.** The build applies one level of Catmull-Clark subdivision to the arms before posing, interpolating the skin weights, so knuckles and fingers are rounded rather than faceted (about 7,200 faces for both arms).

**Materials.** `AuthoredViewModel` replaces the arm material with a subsurface node material (`MeshSSSNodeMaterial`). Skin is told apart from cloth by colour, since it is redder than it is green where gloves, sleeves and the watch strap are grey or grey-green. Cloth stays rough with a soft sheen at grazing angles, like fabric nap, and a stronger normal map. Skin is pulled part way toward its own brightness, because the painted texture is far more saturated than real skin and read as orange under the lamps, and gets a faint sheen of fine hair. Light passing through thin skin comes out red, so fingers and the edge of the forearm glow when a lamp is behind them.

**Light.** The arms and weapon draw in their own scene so they never sink into walls, which means the world's lights cannot reach them. `ViewLighting` (`src/game/viewLighting.ts`) lights them like the spot the player is in. From the market's 90 light sources, whichever renderer is in use, it ranks each lamp's irradiance at the hands. The strongest becomes a directional key light with a 1024 px shadow map around the arms, so fingers and the weapon shade each other. The next four become point lights placed where they are relative to the camera. The rest add a little to the ambient light in their own colour, which otherwise matches the world's sky and ground. Eight times a second, rays from the hands to the strongest eight lamps and to the sun check what is blocked; a blocked lamp fades to 12% over about a sixth of a second, and the low sun counts only where it reaches the player. Each shot lights the hands from the muzzle for a few hundredths of a second.

### Building the models

The models are built by `scripts/viewmodels/build.py`, a Blender (4.4 or newer; built with 5.2) script run by `npm run build:viewmodels` after `scripts/viewmodels/fetch.sh` downloads the sources into the ignored `assets-src/` folder. The script cuts the body mesh to the faces weighted to the arm bones, keeps the arm chains plus their spine ancestors, re-tails the Biped bones so each finger segment has a clean flexion axis, subdivides the arms, crops the two arm islands out of the 2K body atlas into one 1200 px texture (painting over the sleeve insignia) and derives roughness from the specular map. Each weapon is joined into one mesh in a canonical frame (barrel or blade along +Y in Blender, grip at the origin) and parented to a weapon root placed in front of the camera. Hands are posed by two-bone IK with a pole target, a copied hand orientation and per-finger curl angles, all described per weapon in the script's `WEAPONS` table; the knife's arm also moves the shoulder (a three-bone chain) so its strokes can reach across and out. The clips are keyed on the weapon root (bob, sway, carry, recoil, or the knife strokes as keyed poses on a Catmull-Rom curve), baked to bone keys at 24 fps, pushed to NLA tracks and exported with WebP textures. The bake reports the worst gap between the right wrist and its target in each clip; every clip now reaches with no gap. `--preview DIR` renders each clip from the game camera plus close-ups of the grip for checking poses without the game.

References: [Blender glTF export](https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/dev/addons/scene_gltf2.html), [Three.js GLTFLoader](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/GLTFLoader.html), [additive animation](https://threejs.org/docs/#api/en/animation/AnimationUtils.makeClipAdditive).

### Other players

`public/models/player.glb` (1.3 MB) is the same Rocketbox soldier as a whole character: about 8,000 triangles, 54 joints, a 1K body atlas and 512 px head, helmet and equipment maps, with `idle`, `walk`, `run` and `crouch` retargeted from the Rocketbox animation library and a generated one-second `death` fall. `scripts/viewmodels/build_player.py` samples each library clip's world-space bone rotations frame by frame (the clip files carry their first frame as the bind pose, so local rotations cannot be copied directly), applies them to the avatar's own skeleton with the pelvis height, blends the last frames of each loop into the first, and places a `weapon_socket` empty on the right hand whose -Z runs along the fingers.

`PlayerModelLibrary` (`src/game/playerModel.ts`) loads the file named by `VITE_PLAYER_MODEL` once; each peer gets a `SkeletonUtils` clone that shares geometry and textures and only clones materials, so the body can carry the peer's colour tint. `RemotePlayers` draws nothing for another player until the model is ready (a failed load leaves them undrawn) and then drives the character from the same pose packets: idle, walk, run and crouch crossfade over 200 ms from `moving`, `running` and `crouching`; `pitch` tilts the upper spine and head about their bind-pose right axis after the mixer runs; each holds a copy of the first-person weapon model once it loads. The knife sits on the hand socket and follows the arm: a slash raises it forward and sweeps it across the body, a stab draws it back and drives it out. A gun is held the way the first-person arms hold it, from other players' view too. At load, `gripOf` (`src/game/weaponModels.ts`) poses a copy of each view model on its idle clip's first frame and records, in the gun's frame, where the right shoulder, both hands and the muzzle are. Each frame `PlayerModelInstance.aimGun` turns the gun along the player's view, with their pitch, and places it so the character's right shoulder sits where the first-person shoulder does: the stock in the shoulder, the barrel where they aim. Both arms then reach for the gun with two-bone inverse kinematics (the law of cosines, the elbow bent down and out to its own side), the right hand on the grip and the left on the handguard of the rifle or wrapped under the pistol. The hands keep their turn on the forearm: the first-person arms' hand turns do not carry over, as the two skeletons' hand bones point different ways. Running, the gun drops 38° and swings 18° across the body to a low ready, easing back in about an eighth of a second. A shot kicks it back 4.5 cm and its muzzle up 7°, settling in about a tenth of a second, and shows the model's own muzzle flash for 50 ms, scaled and rolled at random. Tracers, the flash's light and spent cases start at that gun's muzzle and ejection port. Before this, the gun hung from the right hand of an arm swinging to the walk, pointed wherever the hand did, and a shot only nudged the arm, so a player could hit you while their gun pointed at the ground; tracers came from in front of their chest. Health zero plays the death clip and leaves the body on the ground until respawn, with empty hands, since the weapon has dropped. Eight characters add roughly 65,000 triangles and no extra textures.

### Weapon switching

First-person geometry renders in its own scene and pass, lit as described above and laid over the world, preserving depth testing between fingers, palms and weapons without clipping against world geometry. Switching lowers the old weapon, changes it below the frame, then raises the new one over 560 ms. Local firing and leader validation both block attacks during this interval. Rapid switches retain a transition instead of snapping the visible model.

### Setup and loading screens

The page opens on the setup screen (`src/main.ts`), which needs only a small first script, its styles and three fonts, about 45 KB of code and styles before the fonts. The player picks single player or a room to create or join, the map, a callsign, a voice and whether to hear 18+ lines and memes; the choices are kept in the browser (`src/settings.ts`) and an invite link opens on its room, ready to join. While the player chooses, the game's code (`src/play.ts`, about 1.7 MB, and Rapier's 3 MB WebAssembly) and the chosen map's files download in the background. A map lists its files by path prefix (`src/maps.ts`), and a Vite plugin lists every public file with its size (`virtual:public-files`), so the download counts real bytes. The downloads are kept as blobs (`src/assetStore.ts`) that Three.js loaders reach through the default loading manager's URL modifier and `fetch` calls through `assetFetch`, so nothing is fetched twice; they are freed 15 s after the world is built. The backdrop of both screens is a still of the chosen map without the HUD or hands, and its card shows a smaller one.

**Deploy** locks the mouse for mouse look: browsers lock it only during a click or key press, so the setup screen locks it then, to the page's own canvas, which the renderer draws on once the world is built. The lock lasts through loading, deaths and respawns; only a dialog frees it, or Esc. With the mouse free in play, a hint under the crosshair says to click, and a click that does not drag locks it again (Chrome refuses for about a second after Esc); dragging still looks around, and a tap on a touch screen attacks. Deploy also shows the loading screen (`src/loading.ts`): any of the map still downloading, then the sounds chosen (the player's language, 18+ lines only if they are on, memes only if they are on), then building the world. Each step owns a share of the bar sized by how long it takes: downloads by their bytes at the speed measured during setup, and the building steps by times measured in Chrome with WebGPU on a Mac, where building the shaders takes about 2.5 s of the 2.8. Steps that report their progress (bytes, clips, shader slices) fill their share as they go; the short building steps creep across theirs over their expected time. The bar and the percentage are drawn from one value each frame, so they always agree; it only moves forward, and the screen stays until both read 100%, then fades. The first frames reveal the market in 16 slices, each building its own shaders, instead of all at once, so the page keeps drawing and the bar follows the slices; that made loading about 8% longer (2.6 s to 2.8 s with the files downloaded) but removed a 2.3 s freeze at a fixed percentage. With the cache disabled at 25 Mbit/s and Deploy pressed 1.5 s after opening, the bar rose steadily through 12 s of download and 4 s of building. A failure stops the tips and shows the error.
