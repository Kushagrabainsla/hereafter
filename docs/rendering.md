# Rendering

## The Night Market's look

**Sky and sun.** The market is set at the end of a wet afternoon, after the look of Saltline, a one-prompt Fable 5.2 shooter: a low sun shines through haze, backlighting the scene. `src/world/duskSky.ts` paints a 2048 × 1024 equirectangular sky at load. It has a slate-blue zenith and a pale, warm band along the horizon. There is a bright glow round the sun and broken rain cloud: dark slate away from the sun, lit gold and rose at the edges toward it. Dithering stops banding. The sky is both the background and the environment that every surface reflects. The sun stands about 26° up in the north-northwest (`MARKET_SUN` in `nightMarket.ts`). It shines down the main alley onto the wet street and across the cross alleys onto the upper floors. A cool, low hemisphere light keeps the shade blue and deep against the warm sunlight. The lamps burn at half their night strength (`LAMP_LEVEL`), so the stalls still glow.

**Air.** `src/world/atmosphere.ts` lays the haze over the finished world, from each pixel's distance and direction, rebuilt from the depth buffer. The haze thickens with distance and near the ground (0.8% per metre at street level, thinning to a third every 9 m up). It is kept thin: a thicker haze turned the alleys milky and lost to the night look in blind tests. It brightens toward the sun with a wide glow and a tight one, so the far end of an alley glows and the near shops stay clear. The sun's disc is drawn on the sky. Light shafts are made in screen space: the open sky round the sun is blurred outward from the sun's place on the screen, so roof edges, cables and signs cut beams through the haze when the player looks toward the sun. Three's shadow-map god rays (`GodraysNode`) returned no light with this shadow setup, so they are not used. A second, wide bloom with a high threshold gives the sun a lens halo. The shafts are drawn at a quarter of the resolution with a 24-tap blur. The rest of the pass costs a few arithmetic operations per pixel.

**Lamp light in the air.** `src/world/lampHaze.ts` makes the outdoor lamps light the damp air. Each lamp draws the inside of a sphere round it in the clouds' pass. At each pixel, the shader sums the lamp's light scattered toward the eye in eight steps along the view ray inside the sphere. It stops at the wall or floor in the world's depth, so a beam ends on the street and a stall roof or a body cuts it. A random offset at each pixel hides the steps. Bare bulbs and lanterns get a tight halo, at most 1.6 m: wide halos on every bulb turned the street milky in tests. The barn lamps on the service alley walls send a cone down the wall, six times stronger than a halo, up to 3.5 m long. The stalls' cage lamps light down and out from under their awnings, only 1.5 times stronger: a strong glow filled each stall like fog. The glow follows each light's strength, so a lamp that is shot out loses its glow too. A blind judge preferred the market with the glow at 5 spots of 8. With the new window frames, it cost about 5% of the frame rate on High and Low at 2560 × 1440 at 2× (1% to 12% in single runs, as the machine slowed during them).

**Rain and wet ground.** `src/world/wetness.ts` decides where water stands. Two reads of the shared noise block, laid flat every 14 m and every 4.5 m, mark about a fifth of the street as separate puddles. A top-down mask of the indoor floors keeps the shops, the restaurant and the bar dry. The street material turns darker and smoother inside a puddle, and rain rings ripple its surface by bump mapping. On High and Medium, screen-space reflections (`SSRNode`, at half resolution) mirror the sunlit facades, lamps and signs in the water. The pass rebuilds each pixel's world position from depth and traces rays only where the puddle mask says there is water. Rain on the water stretches each reflection into a streak toward the eye, as on any wet street: the reflection is smeared up and down the screen over about 5% of its height in twelve taps, each pixel's taps offset by a different fraction of a step so they blend into one smear. That also hides the stair-steps the half-resolution pass leaves on reflected buildings and signs. A blur in every direction (a rougher pass with finer steps) lost a blind test as ghost images; the streaks won 10 spots of 18 and lost none (wet ground 6 against 3), at about 3% of the frame rate on High. The game reads the same mask on the CPU, so footsteps splash in puddles. `src/world/rain.ts` draws the rain on the graphics card from each drop's number: 14,000 drops on High, 10,000 on Medium and 6,000 on Low. Each drop has a fixed place that repeats round the player, falls at 8.5 m/s with a little wind, and is drawn as a thin streak 30 to 55 cm long, the distance it falls while a camera's shutter is open. The rain draws in the clouds' pass, after ambient occlusion and reflections: in the world pass, each streak and splash also wrote the normal buffer and left dark lines and squares on what was behind it. The world's depth hides it behind walls. A streak shows little against shade and lights up with the sun behind it, as a real drop does, so the rain is seen mostly toward the sun. Drops nearer than 2.5 m are left out, and none fall indoors. Small rings mark where drops hit the street near the player. The rain sound is a synthesized hiss and patter, muffled under a roof.

**Lights.** On WebGPU, Three.js `ClusteredLighting` (Forward+ clustered shading) sorts lights into screen tiles and depth slices with a compute pass (a fixed 40 × 24 grid of tiles and 16 slices, so the grid never changes with the screen; see Resolution below), so each pixel shades only the lights that reach it and the shader does not grow with the light count. The market has 93 real point lights: three along each bulb string, one under each stall awning, two per lantern row, every wall lamp, a spill of light outside each lit shop, one beside each neon sign, and the lights of the restaurant, the bar, the shops and the food truck. WebGL 2 cannot run the clustering pass, so it keeps six key lights and draws soft pools of light on the ground instead. Lamps can be shot out (`src/world/lamps.ts`): a broken lamp's glow moves to a dark copy or its sign's emission drops, and each real light shines in proportion to the lamps still feeding it. On WebGL each key light follows the lamps within 7 m of it. The first-person lighting reads the same intensities, so hands go dark in a darkened alley too. Without clustering, an earlier test with 31 lights took more than nine seconds to build its shaders.

**Baked light.** Real-time lights shine straight onto surfaces, but light that bounces, and the way corners, the ground under awnings and the foot of narrow alleys see less of the sky, has to be computed offline. `npm run build:lightmaps` (`scripts/lightmaps/`) does it from the map's own code. A tool page builds the market in headless Chrome, flattens every static surface into a 2048 px lightmap atlas at about 15 texels per metre, and leaves out faces no player can see (rooftops, undersides, faces buried in or flush against other solids, and outer walls facing away from the market). Blender's Cycles then bakes two maps on the GPU, in about ten minutes on an Apple M4: sky visibility (ambient occlusion within 6 m) and light from the 90 lamps and the low sun after at least one bounce, with the stalls, the food truck and the rest of the kit (fire escapes, air conditioners, the record shop's fittings) as occluders. Doors move, so they are left out. Each chart is smoothed on its own, and the bounce map is scaled and sRGB-encoded. The 0.5 MB of WebP images and the atlas coordinates (`public/lightmaps/`) load with the textures. `src/world/lightmap.ts` gives the static surfaces those coordinates. Sky visibility then occludes their sky and environment light, so rooms lose the cold sky fill on their walls and wet paving stops mirroring the violet sky inside covered spots. The bounce is added in proportion to each surface's colour, lifted 1.6 times to stand in for light from signs, lit windows and lanterns, which the bake leaves out. A checksum of each surface's geometry is stored with the bake, so a map changed without rebaking keeps real-time light only and warns in the console.

**Rain stains.** A third baked map, the drip mask, marks where rain runs off sills, ledges, cornices, air conditioners and signs down the wall below them. Blender bakes each texel's world position and normal, then casts rays straight up from every wall texel through a BVH of the whole scene (`bake_drip` in `bake.py`, about 6 million rays). Each texel stores how near the thing above it is, within 1.6 m. The rays start 2 cm and 10 cm out on both sides of the wall, because a window unit stands a little proud of its wall, with a gap behind it. `npm run build:lightmaps` bakes it with the rest; `bake.py -- --drip-only` bakes it alone in about a minute. The wall shader (`lightmap.ts`) cuts the mask into columns of different lengths with noise, adds a faint even stain under every ledge and leaves broad patches cleaner. A stain darkens the wall by up to 45% and makes it 35% smoother, as wet grime is. Walls over an indoor floor stay clean. Earlier tries at grime from world-space noise could not follow sills and pipes and lost in blind tests; this one won 5 spots of 8.

**A lightmap fix.** Until November 2026 the game read all the lightmaps upside down. Blender writes the atlas with its first row at the top of the image, but the game loaded the images with three's default vertical flip, so every surface sampled its sky shadowing and bounce light from another chart. The maps are smooth, so nobody saw it until a test bake of height bands showed it. The bake's smoothing also used upside-down chart rectangles. Both are fixed: the game reads the images as stored, and the bake turns the export's rectangles to Blender's row order.

**Glow and grading.** The world renders once into a half-float target and the first-person view into its own multisampled (4×) pass, in a `RenderPipeline`. Ground-truth ambient occlusion (GTAO) runs at half resolution from the world's depth, rebuilding normals from it rather than writing a normal buffer, and darkens contact and creases the lightmap is too coarse for, and anything that moves. SMAA then smooths the world's edges, since the effect needs a plain depth buffer that multisampling would not give it. SMAA finds edges by differences in colour, and in linear light the night market is so dark that a dim door frame against a dark wall differed by less than its threshold, so most edges went unsmoothed; it now sees the world at the game's exposure and gamma-encoded, as the eye does, and its result is decoded back. At a 16-inch MacBook's size that turned stepped diagonals such as the table braces into smooth lines and took about 4% of the hard steps out of the frame. The view is laid over the world by its own coverage, since both clear to transparent; drawing straight to the canvas after the pipeline would replace its output. Bloom (strength 0.75, radius 0.55, threshold 0.9) makes bulbs, lanterns, neon tubes, lit windows and muzzle flashes glow. A grade cools the shadows, warms the highlights and adds a touch of saturation, a soft vignette darkens the corners, ACES tone mapping and the sRGB conversion follow, and fine film grain over the result breaks up banding and the too-clean look of rendered surfaces. Screen-space reflections once covered all the wet paving and cut the frame rate at Retina resolution to a third. They now run only on standing water (see Rain and wet ground). On WebGPU, one more clustered light flares at each muzzle and each grenade burst, so shots and blasts light the alley for a moment.

**Surfaces.** Walls use node materials that darken toward the ground over the first three metres and in broad, noisy patches, so facades look weathered rather than evenly tiled. Paving and asphalt carry puddles from world-space fractal noise: inside a puddle the surface turns dark and nearly mirror-smooth, so it picks up the lights and the sky. Every window is a modelled unit set on the wall: a moulded architrave round a deep opening, an inner casing, a dark reveal, sash bars with eased edges, a chamfered stone sill on corbels and a lintel with a cap and a keystone; its glass is a card in front of the reveal. The architrave is one profile of twelve points swept round the opening and mitred at the corners. Each change of slope catches the light differently, so the frame reads as moulded from the street, where the earlier flat boards read as cut card. Small chamfers alone were tried before and no judge saw them at play distance. The back doors' canopies are corrugated galvanized sheet, not a flat white slab. A cornice runs along the top of each facade and a string course under its windows, and roller shutters close the passages at the alleys' ends. Unlit panes are dark glass reflecting the dusk, and lit ones show one of eight painted rooms: curtains, blinds, a television's blue light, a plant, a kitchen, a lamp, a half curtain or a pink-lit room. Neon signs are glass tubes along the letters and a border on a dark backer, so only the tubes glow; shop, kitchen and banner signs are lit boxes with raised letters. Six vertical blade signs hang over the alleys. Paper posters, taped up and peeling at a corner, and a sprayed mural cover parts of the service alley. Downpipes, air conditioners, condensers, meters, vents, back doors, fire escapes and the stalls with their goods come from a modelled kit (`src/world/kit.ts`), one instanced mesh per piece and kept-apart material: every plain material of a piece is merged into one mesh whose vertices carry each part's colour, roughness and metalness, so a piece of many materials draws in one call ([asset pipeline](asset-pipeline.md)). The kit and the pipeline's families are compressed with meshopt and unpacked on load. The lanterns, bulbs, lamps and signs are modelled pieces from the pipeline too; neon signs are glass tubes bent into the letters, lit by the game. Each stall's torn awning and collapsed pile are instances too, kept at zero scale until they are needed. The grill stalls' coals glow, so bloom picks them up. Every kit piece casts a sun shadow, since the sun now reaches the street. Smoke, steam and powder are soft particles in a scene of their own (`steamPipes.ts`). Each billow reads the world pass's depth at its screen position and fades over its last 0.9 m in front of what is behind it, which also hides it behind walls without a depth test. The cloud pass is laid over the world after ambient occlusion and SMAA, by its own coverage like the hands. Drawn in the world pass, the occlusion would trace the creases of the walls behind a cloud in dark lines across it. Billows around the eye cannot cover the view the way a real cloud does, so the viewer also measures how thick the clouds are at the eye and lays up to 90% grey haze over the world, under the hands. three's own soft-particle helper names a GLSL reserved word, so the fade is written out for WebGL to compile.

**Detail and motion.** Cedar water tanks on steel stands and TV antennas break the roofline against the sky, cables sag across both alleys, and the food truck is a full model (see [the Night Market](night-market.md#the-food-truck)) with weathered paint that darkens toward the road. Lanterns sway, the bar's OPEN sign stutters, and steam rises from the food truck's roof fan, a stall and a manhole.

**Cost.** Measured in headless Chrome on an Apple M4 at the spawn, with about 430 draw calls and 263,000 triangles. The baked light and post-processing were measured while the machine was heavily loaded (load average 20 to 40), against the previous release at the same time:

| Resolution | Previous release | Now |
|---|---|---|
| 1440 × 813 | 60 FPS (the frame cap) | 60 FPS |
| 2880 × 1626, a Retina screen's pixel count | 32 FPS | 24 to 27 FPS |
| 1440 × 813 on WebGL 2 (`?renderer=webgl`) | 34 FPS | 40 FPS (no multisampling of the world) |

On an idle machine the previous release held 60 FPS at Retina resolution and 33 to 39 at 4K; GTAO, SMAA and grain together cost about a fifth of the frame there. **Resolution.** The world renders at most 4.2 million pixels, up to twice the CSS resolution. A 16-inch MacBook Pro's Retina screen gets 1.48×, a 13-inch Air 1.73× and a 5K display 1.07×. Changing the drawing size used to rebuild every lit shader, because three's clustered lighting writes its grid of screen tiles into each one. On a 16-inch Retina screen each rebuild froze the page for up to 13 s. The old renderer also lowered its resolution a quarter step at a time whenever the frame rate dipped, so a large screen froze again and again just after loading. An earlier note blamed headless emulation for the 2 FPS this gave at a 2× device scale; that was this freeze. Now `src/world/fixedClusters.ts` keeps the grid at 40 × 24 tiles at every size and finds each fragment's tile through a uniform, so no resize rebuilds a shader. The size is chosen when the window settles, 0.3 s after the last resize. After four seconds under 40 FPS the budget drops to 55%, at most twice, and never climbs back, but never below the CSS resolution: on a 16-inch MacBook the second drop used to reach 0.81×, where every edge turned to blocks of pixels, and now stops at 1×. If the graphics device is lost, the loading card returns with the error instead of a frozen frame.

| Emulated screen (CSS size at 2×) | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| 13-inch Air, 1470 × 956 | 4 to 14 FPS, frames frozen up to 9.4 s | 48 FPS, worst frame 50 ms |
| 16-inch Pro, 1728 × 1117 | 2 to 4 FPS, up to 12.9 s | 48 FPS, worst 34 ms |
| 5K display, 2560 × 1440 | 1 to 1.5 FPS, up to 23 s | 49 FPS, worst 33 ms |
| Dragging the window wider, then fullscreen | 2.5 s freeze per resize, after coarser tiles | worst frame 50 ms |

**Many players.** With eleven remote players in view, the main thread's time goes almost entirely to three preparing and submitting draws, not to the GPU or the network. On a CPU slowed threefold, a crowded room dropped from 57 to 34 FPS. About 690 objects passed through the renderer each frame, 300 of them through its full per-object update. Half of those came from redrawing the sun's shadow map. Two changes cut this:

- **The sun's shadow map is redrawn every fourth frame.** Its shadow covers the whole map from a fixed place (`shadow.autoUpdate` off, `SHADOW_EVERY` in `mapViewer.ts`). It used to follow the camera and be redrawn every frame, with every remote player in it. For a while it was drawn only once, when the sun lit only the upper floors; now that the sun reaches the street, shoved props, doors and players must cast moving shadows.
- **Remote players out of view are neither drawn nor animated.** three never culls the characters, because their skinned bounds are unreliable, so `RemotePlayers.update` tests each one against the view itself. Players beyond 18 m animate every second frame and beyond 35 m every third, with the skipped time caught up.

WebGPU render bundles (`BundleGroup`) were tried for the static scenery and made it slower: three r186 still runs its full update for every bundled object each frame, so a bundle saves only the GPU command encoding.

| Renderer CPU per frame, median of three alternating runs | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Alone | 13.7 ms | 10.0 ms |
| Eleven players running in view | 21.0 ms | 16.3 ms |
| Eleven players firing carbines in view | 20.3 ms | 17.4 ms |
| Eleven players firing behind the camera | 6.6 ms | 4.2 ms |

These runs shared the machine with other applications, so single runs varied up to threefold; the new build was faster in ten of twelve paired runs. Most of the remaining cost with everyone firing is the effects of each shot.

**Graphics presets.** `src/world/quality.ts` defines three presets, and the setup screen offers Auto, High, Medium and Low. No preset lowers the drawing resolution. Each preset trims the cost of every pixel instead:

| Preset | What it turns off | Mean FPS, 2560 × 1440 at 2× |
|---|---|---|
| High | Nothing | about 26 |
| Medium | Ambient occlusion | 31 (16% faster) |
| Low | Ambient occlusion, reflections in the puddles, and half the multisampling of the hands | 31 against High's 24 to 26 (in a later run) |

These figures are means of three spots in alternating runs, measured against High before and after each one. The machine slowed during the runs, so only runs next to each other can be compared. Turned off one at a time, the costs were:

- The lamps as real lights: 4 FPS.
- Ambient occlusion: 4.5 FPS. Half its samples saved nothing.
- Edge smoothing: 1.7 FPS.
- The sun's shadows: 1.5 FPS.
- Multisampling of the hands: 1 FPS.
- Glow and grain: nothing.

Every preset keeps the lamps and the edge smoothing. Without the lamps the stalls and the service alley go dark: the six key lights of WebGL 2 ran 52% faster but lost the market's light. Without edge smoothing the edges stair-step. Every preset also keeps the sun's shadows: the sun now reaches the wet street, and without its shadows on Low the whole street was sunlit and the puddles blazed white toward the sun. They cost Low about 8%. Puddle reflections stay off on Low, and a cap on their brightness keeps the haze round the sun from mirroring into a white glare on the others. With everything off, the frame rate reached only 43.5 FPS. The rest of the frame is the lit world itself.

**Auto.** Auto picks High when the world draws 3 million pixels or fewer, and Medium on a larger or high-density screen. After ten seconds of play, Auto measures the frame rate for fifteen seconds. If the mean is under 45 FPS, Auto saves the next cheaper preset for the next match and tells the player in a toast. A change during a match would rebuild every shader and stall the game. The saved preset applies only to screens with at least 80% of the pixels it was measured on. Thus a laptop that ran slowly on a large external display starts again on its own screen. The FPS label in the game shows the preset in use.

## Approach

Each player renders the same bundled map locally and sends pose and combat data over WebRTC; peers do not stream geometry or animation bones. [Rapier 3D](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/getting_started_js/) runs character physics and scene queries in WebAssembly. Before claiming 2–8 player performance, test several real MacBooks and Windows GPUs in a live room, record frame-time percentiles and memory use, and inspect worst-case views with other players and weapon effects visible.

Modern FPS weapon handling benefits from responsive camera and view-model motion: turn settling, stance transitions, sway, and near-wall behaviour are described in [Call of Duty's movement notes](https://www.callofduty.com/blog/2026/08/call-of-duty-modern-warfare-4-next-highlights-multiplayer-gameplay-systems). The first-person arms and the other players come from a licensed, rigged character asset with blended clips (below). [Three.js AnimationMixer](https://threejs.org/manual/pages/animation-system.html) crossfades the locomotion clips.

### Authored first-person assets

`public/models/hands.glb`, `pistol.glb` and `rifle.glb` are the bundled first-person view models: the arms, gloves and wristwatch of the Microsoft Rocketbox avatar `Military_Male_03` (MIT) holding the Poly Haven [Service Pistol](https://polyhaven.com/a/service_pistol) (CC0) and the OpenGameArt [AKM](https://opengameart.org/content/akm) by LonesomeDucky (CC0). `hands.glb` is the bare arms, for carrying props and the extinguisher. It is still built from the old knife rig with the Poly Haven [Fish Knife](https://polyhaven.com/a/fish_knife) (CC0), and the game removes the blade node (`weapon`) when it loads the file. Sources, authors and licences are recorded in [data sources](data-sources.md). The files are 1.5–1.8 MB each with WebP textures: the arms share one repacked 1200 px atlas that keeps the source's own detail (each arm's island is about 600 × 1100 px in the 2K body atlas), and the AKM's 4K maps are reduced to 1K. KTX2/Basis would cut their GPU memory further but needs the Basis transcoder shipped with the site, so it is left for the general texture pass.

`AuthoredViewModel` (`src/game/authoredViewModel.ts`) loads the same-origin GLB named by `VITE_HANDS_VIEWMODEL`, `VITE_PISTOL_VIEWMODEL` and `VITE_RIFLE_VIEWMODEL` (set in the committed `.env`, so dev, `build` and `build:pages` all bundle them). Each file is authored in camera-local metres (+Y up, forward -Z) and attached directly to the first-person camera without rescaling. Clips are named `idle`, `walk`, `run` and `fire`. Locomotion clips crossfade over 150 ms. Attack clips start on the neutral pose and are converted to additive clips at load, so recoil and the muzzle flash layer over whichever locomotion clip is playing. The loader clones each attack clip first, because exporters share one accessor between identical constant tracks and the additive conversion subtracts in place. A node named `muzzle` carries the flash geometry (scaled to nothing outside the fire clip) and gives the tracer its start point. The spring sway and breathing ([movement notes](movement.md#how-the-held-weapon-moves)) and the lower-and-raise weapon switch come from the procedural `WeaponVisuals` state (`dynamics()`). A model that is missing or fails to load is not drawn: there are no stand-in hands or guns.

The files hold each gun yawed 13 to 15° toward the screen centre and canted slightly. Its barrel therefore crossed the line of sight at one range and pointed off to the side everywhere else, while tracers left the muzzle for the crosshair, so the gun looked tilted against its own shots. At load, `straighten` turns the whole arms-and-weapon rig about the grip by the inverse of the weapon's rest rotation in the idle clip. The barrel then runs parallel to the view axis. Lines parallel to the view axis meet at the screen centre, so the barrel points at the crosshair from every range and the tracer continues it. In headless Chrome, both guns' barrel lines met the screen within 2 px of the crosshair once raised, and the idle sway moved them by less than half a degree. The bare arms keep their authored angle.

`src/game/weaponModels.ts` loads each file once. The first-person view uses the whole file. Pickups and other players use a copy of just the gun's `weapon` node without the muzzle flash, sharing geometry and textures, so every weapon in the game is the same model. A pickup spins the model about its centre, drawn 1.6 times life size for the pistol and 1.05 times for the carbine so it reads at a distance. Until the files load, a pickup shows only its stand and characters hold nothing.

### Making the hands look real

The source avatar's hands are a game asset of about 1,200 triangles an arm, and the first version lit them with a fixed studio light, so they looked like grey plastic in front of a warm, lamp-lit alley. Three things changed.

**Shape.** The build applies one level of Catmull-Clark subdivision to the arms before posing, interpolating the skin weights, so knuckles and fingers are rounded rather than faceted (about 7,200 faces for both arms).

**Materials.** `AuthoredViewModel` replaces the arm material with a subsurface node material (`MeshSSSNodeMaterial`). Skin is told apart from cloth by colour, since it is redder than it is green where gloves, sleeves and the watch strap are grey or grey-green. Cloth stays rough with a soft sheen at grazing angles, like fabric nap, and a stronger normal map. Skin is pulled part way toward its own brightness, because the painted texture is far more saturated than real skin and read as orange under the lamps, and gets a faint sheen of fine hair. Light passing through thin skin comes out red, so fingers and the edge of the forearm glow when a lamp is behind them.

**Light.** The arms and weapon draw in their own scene so they never sink into walls, which means the world's lights cannot reach them. `ViewLighting` (`src/game/viewLighting.ts`) lights them like the spot the player is in. From the market's 90 light sources, whichever renderer is in use, it ranks each lamp's irradiance at the hands. The strongest becomes a directional key light with a 1024 px shadow map around the arms, so fingers and the weapon shade each other. The next four become point lights placed where they are relative to the camera. The rest add a little to the ambient light in their own colour, which otherwise matches the world's sky and ground. Eight times a second, rays from the hands to the strongest eight lamps and to the sun check what is blocked; a blocked lamp fades to 12% over about a sixth of a second, and the low sun counts only where it reaches the player. Each shot lights the hands from the muzzle for a few hundredths of a second.

### Building the models

The models are built by `scripts/viewmodels/build.py`, a Blender (4.4 or newer; built with 5.2) script run by `npm run build:viewmodels` after `scripts/viewmodels/fetch.sh` downloads the sources into the ignored `assets-src/` folder. The script cuts the body mesh to the faces weighted to the arm bones, keeps the arm chains plus their spine ancestors, re-tails the Biped bones so each finger segment has a clean flexion axis, subdivides the arms, crops the two arm islands out of the 2K body atlas into one 1200 px texture (painting over the sleeve insignia) and derives roughness from the specular map. Each weapon is joined into one mesh in a canonical frame (barrel or blade along +Y in Blender, grip at the origin) and parented to a weapon root placed in front of the camera. Hands are posed by two-bone IK with a pole target, a copied hand orientation and per-finger curl angles, all described per weapon in the script's `WEAPONS` table. `scripts/viewmodels/build-all.sh` still builds `hands.glb` from the script's `knife` entry, whose arm also moves the shoulder (a three-bone chain). The clips are keyed on the weapon root (bob, sway, carry and recoil), baked to bone keys at 24 fps, pushed to NLA tracks and exported with WebP textures. The bake reports the worst gap between the right wrist and its target in each clip; every clip now reaches with no gap. `--preview DIR` renders each clip from the game camera plus close-ups of the grip for checking poses without the game.

References: [Blender glTF export](https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/dev/addons/scene_gltf2.html), [Three.js GLTFLoader](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/GLTFLoader.html), [additive animation](https://threejs.org/docs/#api/en/animation/AnimationUtils.makeClipAdditive).

### Other players

`public/models/player.glb` (1.3 MB) is the same Rocketbox soldier as a whole character: about 8,000 triangles, 54 joints, a 1K body atlas and 512 px head, helmet and equipment maps, with `idle`, `walk`, `run` and `crouch` retargeted from the Rocketbox animation library and a generated one-second `death` fall. `scripts/viewmodels/build_player.py` samples each library clip's world-space bone rotations frame by frame (the clip files carry their first frame as the bind pose, so local rotations cannot be copied directly), applies them to the avatar's own skeleton with the pelvis height, blends the last frames of each loop into the first, and places a `weapon_socket` empty on the right hand whose -Z runs along the fingers.

`PlayerModelLibrary` (`src/game/playerModel.ts`) loads the file named by `VITE_PLAYER_MODEL` once; each peer gets a `SkeletonUtils` clone that shares geometry and textures and only clones materials, so the body can carry the peer's colour tint. `RemotePlayers` draws nothing for another player until the model is ready (a failed load leaves them undrawn) and then drives the character from the same pose packets: idle, walk, run and crouch crossfade over 200 ms from `moving`, `running` and `crouching`; `pitch` tilts the upper spine and head about their bind-pose right axis after the mixer runs; each holds a copy of the first-person weapon model once it loads. A gun is held the way the first-person arms hold it, from other players' view too. At load, `gripOf` (`src/game/weaponModels.ts`) poses a copy of each view model on its idle clip's first frame and records, in the gun's frame, where the right shoulder, both hands and the muzzle are. Each frame `PlayerModelInstance.aimGun` turns the gun along the player's view, with their pitch, and places it so the character's right shoulder sits where the first-person shoulder does: the stock in the shoulder, the barrel where they aim. Both arms then reach for the gun with two-bone inverse kinematics (the law of cosines, the elbow bent down and out to its own side), the right hand on the grip and the left on the handguard of the rifle or wrapped under the pistol. The hands keep their turn on the forearm: the first-person arms' hand turns do not carry over, as the two skeletons' hand bones point different ways. Running, the gun drops 38° and swings 18° across the body to a low ready, easing back in about an eighth of a second. A shot kicks it back 4.5 cm and its muzzle up 7°, settling in about a tenth of a second, and shows the model's own muzzle flash for 50 ms, scaled and rolled at random. Tracers, the flash's light and spent cases start at that gun's muzzle and ejection port. Before this, the gun hung from the right hand of an arm swinging to the walk, pointed wherever the hand did, and a shot only nudged the arm, so a player could hit you while their gun pointed at the ground; tracers came from in front of their chest. Health zero plays the death clip and leaves the body on the ground until respawn, with empty hands, since the weapon has dropped. Eight characters add roughly 65,000 triangles and no extra textures.

### Weapon switching

First-person geometry renders in its own scene and pass, lit as described above and laid over the world, preserving depth testing between fingers, palms and weapons without clipping against world geometry. Switching lowers the old weapon, changes it below the frame, then raises the new one over 560 ms. Local firing and leader validation both block attacks during this interval. Rapid switches retain a transition instead of snapping the visible model.

### Setup and loading screens

The page opens on the setup screen (`src/main.ts`), which needs only a small first script, its styles and three fonts, about 45 KB of code and styles before the fonts. The player picks single player or a room to create or join, the map, a callsign, a voice and whether to hear 18+ lines and memes; the choices are kept in the browser (`src/settings.ts`) and an invite link opens on its room, ready to join. While the player chooses, the game's code (`src/play.ts`, about 1.7 MB, and Rapier's 3 MB WebAssembly) and the chosen map's files download in the background. A map lists its files by path prefix (`src/maps.ts`), and a Vite plugin lists every public file with its size (`virtual:public-files`), so the download counts real bytes. The downloads are kept as blobs (`src/assetStore.ts`) that Three.js loaders reach through the default loading manager's URL modifier and `fetch` calls through `assetFetch`, so nothing is fetched twice; they are freed 15 s after the world is built. The backdrop of both screens is a still of the chosen map without the HUD or hands, and its card shows a smaller one.

**Deploy** locks the mouse for mouse look: browsers lock it only during a click or key press, so the setup screen locks it then, to the page's own canvas, which the renderer draws on once the world is built. The lock lasts through loading, deaths and respawns; only a dialog frees it, or Esc. With the mouse free in play, a hint under the crosshair says to click, and a click that does not drag locks it again (Chrome refuses for about a second after Esc); dragging still looks around, and a tap on a touch screen attacks. Deploy and the click back into play also put the game in full screen (`fillScreen` in `src/mouseLock.ts`). The whole page fills the screen, not the canvas, so the HUD and dialogs stay in view. Esc leaves full screen along with the mouse lock, and the click back into play returns to both; a dialog frees the mouse but keeps full screen. The mouse is locked first: going full screen uses up the click, and in headless Chrome a lock asked for after it was refused. Going full screen changes the drawing size, which rebuilds no shader (see Resolution above). Deploy also shows the loading screen (`src/loading.ts`): any of the map still downloading, then the sounds chosen (the player's language, 18+ lines only if they are on, memes only if they are on), then building the world. Each step owns a share of the bar sized by how long it takes: downloads by their bytes at the speed measured during setup, and the building steps by times measured in Chrome with WebGPU on a Mac, where building the shaders takes about 2.5 s of the 2.8. Steps that report their progress (bytes, clips, shader slices) fill their share as they go; the short building steps creep across theirs over their expected time. The bar and the percentage are drawn from one value each frame, so they always agree; it only moves forward, and the screen stays until both read 100%, then fades. The first frames reveal the market in 16 slices, each building its own shaders, instead of all at once, so the page keeps drawing and the bar follows the slices; that made loading about 8% longer (2.6 s to 2.8 s with the files downloaded) but removed a 2.3 s freeze at a fixed percentage. With the cache disabled at 25 Mbit/s and Deploy pressed 1.5 s after opening, the bar rose steadily through 12 s of download and 4 s of building. A failure stops the tips and shows the error.
