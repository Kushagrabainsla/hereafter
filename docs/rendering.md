# Rendering

## The Night Market's look

**Sky.** `src/world/duskSky.ts` paints a 2048 × 1024 equirectangular dusk sky at load: a deep blue zenith fading through violet and rose to an orange band where the sun has just gone, a warm glow toward the sun's bearing, low clouds lit from below, faint city haze over the rooftops, 1,800 stars where the sky has darkened, and dithering against banding. It is both the background and the environment every surface reflects. A low orange sun, about 15° up, catches the upper floors and casts the only shadows; the alleys below are lit by the market itself. A cool hemisphere light and a violet haze fill the shadows.

**Lights.** On WebGPU, Three.js `ClusteredLighting` (Forward+ clustered shading) sorts lights into screen tiles and depth slices with a compute pass, so each pixel shades only the lights that reach it and the shader does not grow with the light count. The market has 90 real point lights: three along each bulb string, one under each stall awning, two per lantern row, every wall lamp, a spill of light outside each lit shop, one beside each neon sign, and the lights of the restaurant, the bar, the record shop and the food truck. WebGL 2 cannot run the clustering pass, so it keeps six key lights and draws soft pools of light on the ground instead. Lamps can be shot out (`src/world/lamps.ts`): a broken lamp's glow moves to a dark copy or its sign's emission drops, and each real light shines in proportion to the lamps still feeding it. On WebGL each key light follows the lamps within 7 m of it. The first-person lighting reads the same intensities, so hands go dark in a darkened alley too. Without clustering, an earlier test with 31 lights took more than nine seconds to build its shaders.

**Baked light.** Real-time lights shine straight onto surfaces, but light that bounces, and the way corners, the ground under awnings and the foot of narrow alleys see less of the sky, has to be computed offline. `npm run build:lightmaps` (`scripts/lightmaps/`) does it from the map's own code. A tool page builds the market in headless Chrome, flattens every static surface into a 2048 px lightmap atlas at about 15 texels per metre, and leaves out faces no player can see (rooftops, undersides, faces buried in or flush against other solids, and outer walls facing away from the market). Blender's Cycles then bakes two maps on the GPU, in about ten minutes on an Apple M4: sky visibility (ambient occlusion within 6 m) and light from the 90 lamps and the low sun after at least one bounce, with the stalls, the food truck and the rest of the kit (fire escapes, air conditioners, the record shop's fittings) as occluders. Doors move, so they are left out. Each chart is smoothed on its own, and the bounce map is scaled and sRGB-encoded. The 0.5 MB of WebP images and the atlas coordinates (`public/lightmaps/`) load with the textures. `src/world/lightmap.ts` gives the static surfaces those coordinates. Sky visibility then occludes their sky and environment light, so rooms lose the cold sky fill on their walls and wet paving stops mirroring the violet sky inside covered spots. The bounce is added in proportion to each surface's colour, lifted 1.6 times to stand in for light from signs, lit windows and lanterns, which the bake leaves out. A checksum of each surface's geometry is stored with the bake, so a map changed without rebaking keeps real-time light only and warns in the console.

**Glow and grading.** The world renders once into a half-float target and the first-person view into its own multisampled (4×) pass, in a `RenderPipeline`. Ground-truth ambient occlusion (GTAO) runs at half resolution from the world's depth, rebuilding normals from it rather than writing a normal buffer, and darkens contact and creases the lightmap is too coarse for, and anything that moves. SMAA then smooths the world's edges, since the effect needs a plain depth buffer that multisampling would not give it. The view is laid over the world by its own coverage, since both clear to transparent; drawing straight to the canvas after the pipeline would replace its output. Bloom (strength 0.75, radius 0.55, threshold 0.9) makes bulbs, lanterns, neon tubes, lit windows and muzzle flashes glow. A grade cools the shadows, warms the highlights and adds a touch of saturation, a soft vignette darkens the corners, ACES tone mapping and the sRGB conversion follow, and fine film grain over the result breaks up banding and the too-clean look of rendered surfaces. Screen-space reflections were tried for the wet paving: with the normal and roughness buffers they need, they cut the frame rate at Retina resolution to a third, so the paving keeps reflecting the sky and the real-time lights instead. On WebGPU, one more clustered light flares at each muzzle and each grenade burst, so shots and blasts light the alley for a moment.

**Surfaces.** Walls use node materials that darken toward the ground over the first three metres and in broad, noisy patches, so facades look weathered rather than evenly tiled. Paving and asphalt carry puddles from world-space fractal noise: inside a puddle the surface turns dark and nearly mirror-smooth, so it picks up the lights and the sky. Window frames have mullions, sills and lintels. Unlit panes are dark glass reflecting the dusk, and lit ones show one of eight painted rooms: curtains, blinds, a television's blue light, a plant, a kitchen, a lamp, a half curtain or a pink-lit room. Neon signs are tubes with a white-hot core on a dark board, so only the letters and border glow; shop, kitchen and banner signs are backlit lightboxes. Six vertical blade signs hang over the alleys, and posters and a mural cover parts of the service alley. Downpipes, air conditioners, condensers, meters, vents, back doors, fire escapes and the stalls with their goods come from a modelled kit (`src/world/kit.ts`), one instanced mesh per piece and material. Each stall's torn awning and collapsed pile are instances too, kept at zero scale until they are needed. The grill stalls' coals glow, so bloom picks them up. Only the fire escapes cast shadows, since only they stand high enough in the low sun. Burst steam is drawn as soft particles: each billow reads the depth behind it and fades over its last 0.9 m, so it never shows a hard line where it meets a wall. three's own soft-particle helper names a GLSL reserved word, so the fade is written out in `steamPipes.ts` for WebGL to compile.

**Detail and motion.** Water tanks and antennas break the roofline against the sky, cables sag across both alleys, and the food truck is a full model (see [the Night Market](night-market.md#the-food-truck)) with weathered paint that darkens toward the road. Lanterns sway, the bar's OPEN sign stutters, and steam rises from the food truck's roof fan, a stall and a manhole.

**Cost.** Measured in headless Chrome on an Apple M4 at the spawn, with about 430 draw calls and 263,000 triangles. The baked light and post-processing were measured while the machine was heavily loaded (load average 20 to 40), against the previous release at the same time:

| Resolution | Previous release | Now |
|---|---|---|
| 1440 × 813 | 60 FPS (the frame cap) | 60 FPS |
| 2880 × 1626, a Retina screen's pixel count | 32 FPS | 24 to 27 FPS |
| 1440 × 813 on WebGL 2 (`?renderer=webgl`) | 34 FPS | 40 FPS (no multisampling of the world) |

On an idle machine the previous release held 60 FPS at Retina resolution and 33 to 39 at 4K; GTAO, SMAA and grain together cost about a fifth of the frame there. The renderer lowers its resolution after sustained slow frames. Emulating a 2× device scale in headless Chrome is itself slow (the previous release measured 2 FPS that way), so those runs are not used.

## Approach

Each player renders the same bundled map locally and sends pose and combat data over WebRTC; peers do not stream geometry or animation bones. [Rapier 3D](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/getting_started_js/) runs character physics and scene queries in WebAssembly. Before claiming 2–8 player performance, test several real MacBooks and Windows GPUs in a live room, record frame-time percentiles and memory use, and inspect worst-case views with other players and weapon effects visible.

Modern FPS weapon handling benefits from responsive camera and view-model motion: turn settling, stance transitions, sway, and near-wall behaviour are described in [Call of Duty's movement notes](https://www.callofduty.com/blog/2026/08/call-of-duty-modern-warfare-4-next-highlights-multiplayer-gameplay-systems). The first-person arms and the other players come from a licensed, rigged character asset with blended clips (below). [Three.js AnimationMixer](https://threejs.org/manual/pages/animation-system.html) crossfades the locomotion clips.

### Authored first-person assets

`public/models/knife.glb`, `pistol.glb` and `rifle.glb` are the bundled first-person view models: the arms, gloves and wristwatch of the Microsoft Rocketbox avatar `Military_Male_03` (MIT) holding the Poly Haven [Fish Knife](https://polyhaven.com/a/fish_knife) and [Service Pistol](https://polyhaven.com/a/service_pistol) (CC0) and the OpenGameArt [AKM](https://opengameart.org/content/akm) by LonesomeDucky (CC0). Sources, authors and licences are recorded in [data sources](data-sources.md). The files are 1.5–1.8 MB each with WebP textures: the arms share one repacked 1200 px atlas that keeps the source's own detail (each arm's island is about 600 × 1100 px in the 2K body atlas), and the AKM's 4K maps are reduced to 1K. KTX2/Basis would cut their GPU memory further but needs the Basis transcoder shipped with the site, so it is left for the general texture pass.

`AuthoredViewModel` (`src/game/authoredViewModel.ts`) loads the same-origin GLB named by `VITE_KNIFE_VIEWMODEL`, `VITE_PISTOL_VIEWMODEL` and `VITE_RIFLE_VIEWMODEL` (set in the committed `.env`, so dev, `build` and `build:pages` all bundle them). Each file is authored in camera-local metres (+Y up, forward -Z) and attached directly to the first-person camera without rescaling. Clips are named `idle`, `walk`, `run` and `fire`, and the knife has `slash` and `stab` instead of `fire`. Locomotion clips crossfade over 150 ms. Attack clips start on the neutral pose and are converted to additive clips at load, so recoil, the knife strokes and the muzzle flash layer over whichever locomotion clip is playing. The knife strokes are authored 2.5 times slower than real time so their fast arcs get enough keyframes, and play 2.5 times faster; the loader clones it first because exporters share one accessor between identical constant tracks and the additive conversion subtracts in place. A node named `muzzle` carries the flash geometry (scaled to nothing outside the fire clip) and gives the tracer its start point. The spring sway and breathing ([movement notes](movement.md#how-the-held-weapon-moves)) and the lower-and-raise weapon switch come from the procedural `WeaponVisuals` state (`dynamics()`), so authored and fallback weapons switch at the same moment and feel the same. A knife stroke all but stops for 75 ms when it bites into a body, and a blade turned by something hard fades out over 160 ms while the hand jolts back on a critically damped spring. A model that is missing or fails to load leaves the procedural hands and weapon for that slot; remove a line from `.env` to use the fallback deliberately.

The files hold each gun yawed 13 to 15° toward the screen centre and canted slightly. Its barrel therefore crossed the line of sight at one range and pointed off to the side everywhere else, while tracers left the muzzle for the crosshair, so the gun looked tilted against its own shots. At load, `straighten` turns the whole arms-and-weapon rig about the grip by the inverse of the weapon's rest rotation in the idle clip. The barrel then runs parallel to the view axis. Lines parallel to the view axis meet at the screen centre, so the barrel points at the crosshair from every range and the tracer continues it. In headless Chrome, both guns' barrel lines met the screen within 2 px of the crosshair once raised, and the idle sway moved them by less than half a degree. The knife keeps its authored angle.

`src/game/weaponModels.ts` loads each file once. The first-person view uses the whole file. Pickups and other players use a copy of just its `weapon` node without the muzzle flash, sharing geometry and textures, so every weapon in the game is the same model. A pickup spins the model about its centre, drawn 1.6 times life size for the pistol and knife and 1.05 times for the carbine so it reads at a distance. Until the files load, pickups and characters show the procedural stand-ins.

### Making the hands look real

The source avatar's hands are a game asset of about 1,200 triangles an arm, and the first version lit them with a fixed studio light, so they looked like grey plastic in front of a warm, lamp-lit alley. Three things changed.

**Shape.** The build applies one level of Catmull-Clark subdivision to the arms before posing, interpolating the skin weights, so knuckles and fingers are rounded rather than faceted (about 7,200 faces for both arms).

**Materials.** `AuthoredViewModel` replaces the arm material with a subsurface node material (`MeshSSSNodeMaterial`). Skin is told apart from cloth by colour, since it is redder than it is green where gloves, sleeves and the watch strap are grey or grey-green. Cloth stays rough with a soft sheen at grazing angles, like fabric nap, and a stronger normal map. Skin is pulled part way toward its own brightness, because the painted texture is far more saturated than real skin and read as orange under the lamps, and gets a faint sheen of fine hair. Light passing through thin skin comes out red, so fingers and the edge of the forearm glow when a lamp is behind them.

**Light.** The arms and weapon draw in their own scene so they never sink into walls, which means the world's lights cannot reach them. `ViewLighting` (`src/game/viewLighting.ts`) lights them like the spot the player is in. From the market's 90 light sources, whichever renderer is in use, it ranks each lamp's irradiance at the hands. The strongest becomes a directional key light with a 1024 px shadow map around the arms, so fingers and the weapon shade each other. The next four become point lights placed where they are relative to the camera. The rest add a little to the ambient light in their own colour, which otherwise matches the world's sky and ground. Eight times a second, rays from the hands to the strongest eight lamps and to the sun check what is blocked; a blocked lamp fades to 12% over about a sixth of a second, and the low sun counts only where it reaches the player. Each shot lights the hands from the muzzle for a few hundredths of a second.

### Building the models

The models are built by `scripts/viewmodels/build.py`, a Blender (4.4 or newer; built with 5.2) script run by `npm run build:viewmodels` after `scripts/viewmodels/fetch.sh` downloads the sources into the ignored `assets-src/` folder. The script cuts the body mesh to the faces weighted to the arm bones, keeps the arm chains plus their spine ancestors, re-tails the Biped bones so each finger segment has a clean flexion axis, subdivides the arms, crops the two arm islands out of the 2K body atlas into one 1200 px texture (painting over the sleeve insignia) and derives roughness from the specular map. Each weapon is joined into one mesh in a canonical frame (barrel or blade along +Y in Blender, grip at the origin) and parented to a weapon root placed in front of the camera. Hands are posed by two-bone IK with a pole target, a copied hand orientation and per-finger curl angles, all described per weapon in the script's `WEAPONS` table; the knife's arm also moves the shoulder (a three-bone chain) so its strokes can reach across and out. The clips are keyed on the weapon root (bob, sway, carry, recoil, or the knife strokes as keyed poses on a Catmull-Rom curve), baked to bone keys at 24 fps, pushed to NLA tracks and exported with WebP textures. The bake reports the worst gap between the right wrist and its target in each clip; every clip now reaches with no gap. `--preview DIR` renders each clip from the game camera plus close-ups of the grip for checking poses without the game.

References: [Blender glTF export](https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/dev/addons/scene_gltf2.html), [Three.js GLTFLoader](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/GLTFLoader.html), [additive animation](https://threejs.org/docs/#api/en/animation/AnimationUtils.makeClipAdditive).

### Other players

`public/models/player.glb` (1.3 MB) is the same Rocketbox soldier as a whole character: about 8,000 triangles, 54 joints, a 1K body atlas and 512 px head, helmet and equipment maps, with `idle`, `walk`, `run` and `crouch` retargeted from the Rocketbox animation library and a generated one-second `death` fall. `scripts/viewmodels/build_player.py` samples each library clip's world-space bone rotations frame by frame (the clip files carry their first frame as the bind pose, so local rotations cannot be copied directly), applies them to the avatar's own skeleton with the pelvis height, blends the last frames of each loop into the first, and places a `weapon_socket` empty on the right hand whose -Z runs along the fingers.

`PlayerModelLibrary` (`src/game/playerModel.ts`) loads the file named by `VITE_PLAYER_MODEL` once; each peer gets a `SkeletonUtils` clone that shares geometry and textures and only clones materials, so the body can carry the peer's colour tint. `RemotePlayers` keeps its primitive mannequin until the model is ready (or forever if it fails) and then drives the character from the same pose packets: idle, walk, run and crouch crossfade over 200 ms from `moving`, `running` and `crouching`; `pitch` tilts the upper spine and head about their bind-pose right axis after the mixer runs; the held weapon sits on the hand socket, as a copy of the first-person weapon model once it loads and the procedural stand-in until then. An attack swings the right arm on top of the animation: a slash raises it forward and sweeps it across the body, a stab draws it back and drives it out, and a shot kicks it up a little. Health zero plays the death clip and leaves the body on the ground until respawn, with empty hands, since the weapon has dropped. Eight characters add roughly 65,000 triangles and no extra textures.

### Weapon grip and switching correction

Procedural hands now attach to the held weapon in weapon-local coordinates. Each grip is described by the gripped part's cross-section, so three-jointed fingers close around the knife handle, the pistol grip and the carbine fore-end, with the index finger on the trigger and an opposing thumb over the top. The pistol uses a supporting second hand over the strong hand; the carbine's support hand sits under the fore-end with its forearm angling down and left. Hands, wrists and forearms inherit the weapon's recoil, sway, landing and sprint motion. These procedural hands are now the fallback behind the authored models above.

Each weapon has a rest pose in `VIEW_POSES` (`src/game/weaponVisuals.ts`). Guns sit low and right of centre and yaw slightly inward so the barrel converges on the crosshair; the carbine's stock leaves the frame at the bottom right instead of filling the view. Gun bodies were slimmed so they read as firearms when seen along their side. Sprinting blends in a per-weapon carry offset that drops the weapon and cants it across the body; it settles back when the player stops running.

First-person geometry renders in its own scene and pass, lit as described above and laid over the world, preserving depth testing between fingers, palms and weapons without clipping against world geometry. Switching lowers the old weapon, changes it below the frame, then raises the new one over 560 ms. Local firing and leader validation both block attacks during this interval. Rapid switches retain a transition instead of snapping the visible model. Headless Chrome checks covered all three grips from the game camera and from side and top views, sprint carries, switch frames, and a scripted pickup, fire, switch and sprint sequence in the running game.

### Loading screen

A full-screen loading card covers the page until the first frame has rendered. The viewer names each step as it starts: loading the market, building alleys and buildings, placing stalls, props and lights, starting the renderer, building physics and preparing shaders for the first frame. Each step owns a span of the bar. Files that finish during a step, which Three.js loaders report to the default loading manager, fill that step's span, so the bar keeps moving on a slow connection. The viewer waits for one paint after naming a step, so the label shows before the blocking work starts. The bar only moves forward, a tip under it changes every 3.5 s, and the card fades out once the game is ready. A failure stops the tips and shows the error on the card. On a connection throttled to 40 Mbit/s with the cache disabled, the dev build filled the arena step from 4% to 45% over about 2.5 s of downloads, then reached 100% about 1.8 s later.
