# Rendering plan and baseline

The pilot now uses one 140 m playable block. Its default asset contains 62 building footprints, 267 road segments, and 8 mapped trees, with a 65 m visual buffer. The source extract contains 291 buildings, 1,927 road segments, and 38 trees. The default JSON is 36,232 bytes versus 223,076 bytes for the full extract. A custom arena URL still loads the full extract.

Buildings and road surfaces are batched by spatial cell. This keeps material reuse while letting Three.js cull cells outside the camera view. Concrete cover uses one simplified detailed model and material set, instanced in small spatial cells at deterministic locations. The same locations are colliders for movement and height-aware combat line of sight. The first-person arms and held weapons are authored, rigged GLB models with idle, walk, run and fire clips; they still respond to turning, landing and weapon switches through the shared procedural state. The sun shadow camera covers the local block more tightly, and the renderer adjusts pixel ratio after sustained low or high frame rates.

On a local headless Chrome run on an Apple M4, the spawn view reported 60 FPS with WebGPU at 1440 × 813 CSS pixels and 2× render resolution (2880 × 1626), with 266 draw calls and about 253,000 rendered triangles. Forcing WebGL 2 with `?renderer=webgl` settled near 52 FPS at 1.75× render resolution in that environment. This is one spawn view and one machine, not a target-device guarantee. The browser performance label exposes live FPS; hover over it for draw calls, triangles, and resolution.

## Why this approach

Modern FPS weapon handling benefits from responsive camera and view-model motion: turn settling, stance transitions, sway, and near-wall behavior are described in [Call of Duty's movement notes](https://www.callofduty.com/blog/2026/08/call-of-duty-modern-warfare-4-next-highlights-multiplayer-gameplay-systems). This build adds turn settling and gait-driven weapon motion. The first-person arms and the other players now come from a licensed, rigged character asset with blended clips (below). [Three.js AnimationMixer](https://threejs.org/manual/pages/animation-system.html) crossfades the locomotion clips and layers the fire clip additively; crouch and aim clips are not authored yet.

The next visual pass should author a small number of high-quality facade, sidewalk, tree, and prop assets with PBR materials. Shared assets should use [instancing](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/InstancedMesh.html), meshes should have near and far levels of detail, and textures should move to [KTX2/Basis compression](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/KTX2Loader.html) after inspecting GPU memory and visual quality. The current barrier model is CC0 [Poly Haven art](https://polyhaven.com/a/concrete_road_barrier_02); its license is recorded in [data sources](data-sources.md).

Each player already renders the same static arena files locally and sends pose and combat data over WebRTC; peers do not stream map geometry or animation bones. Shared URLs and browser caching serve the same asset bytes to different players, while each device still performs its own rendering. [Rapier 3D](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/getting_started_js/) now runs character physics and scene queries in WebAssembly. Rendering remains on the GPU; a separate WASM mesh or texture pipeline would need a measured CPU bottleneck.

Before claiming 2–8 player performance or higher fidelity, test several real MacBooks and Windows GPUs in a live room, record frame-time percentiles and memory use, and inspect worst-case views with other players and weapon effects visible.

### Authored first-person assets

`public/models/knife.glb`, `pistol.glb` and `rifle.glb` are the bundled first-person view models: the arms, gloves and wristwatch of the Microsoft Rocketbox avatar `Military_Male_03` (MIT) holding the Poly Haven [Fish Knife](https://polyhaven.com/a/fish_knife) and [Service Pistol](https://polyhaven.com/a/service_pistol) (CC0) and the OpenGameArt [AKM](https://opengameart.org/content/akm) by LonesomeDucky (CC0). Sources, authors and licences are recorded in [data sources](data-sources.md). The files are 1.0–1.4 MB each with 1K WebP textures; the arms share one repacked atlas, the AKM's 4K maps are reduced to 1K. KTX2/Basis would cut their GPU memory further but needs the Basis transcoder shipped with the site, so it is left for the general texture pass.

`AuthoredViewModel` (`src/game/authoredViewModel.ts`) loads the same-origin GLB named by `VITE_KNIFE_VIEWMODEL`, `VITE_PISTOL_VIEWMODEL` and `VITE_RIFLE_VIEWMODEL` (set in the committed `.env`, so dev, `build` and `build:pages` all bundle them). Each file is authored in camera-local metres (+Y up, forward -Z) and attached directly to the first-person camera without rescaling. Clips are named `idle`, `walk`, `run` and `fire`. Locomotion clips crossfade over 150 ms. The fire clip starts on the neutral pose and is converted to an additive clip at load, so recoil, the knife thrust and the muzzle flash layer over whichever locomotion clip is playing; the loader clones it first because exporters share one accessor between identical constant tracks and the additive conversion subtracts in place. A node named `muzzle` carries the flash geometry (scaled to nothing outside the fire clip) and gives the tracer its start point. Turn settling, landing dips and the lower-and-raise weapon switch come from the procedural `WeaponVisuals` state (`dynamics()`), so authored and fallback weapons switch at the same moment and feel the same. A model that is missing or fails to load leaves the procedural hands and weapon for that slot; remove a line from `.env` to use the fallback deliberately.

The models are built by `scripts/viewmodels/build.py`, a Blender (4.4 or newer; built with 5.2) script run by `npm run build:viewmodels` after `scripts/viewmodels/fetch.sh` downloads the sources into the ignored `assets-src/` folder. The script cuts the body mesh to the faces weighted to the arm bones, keeps the arm chains plus their spine ancestors, re-tails the Biped bones so each finger segment has a clean flexion axis, crops the two arm islands out of the 2K body atlas into one 1K texture (painting over the sleeve insignia) and derives roughness from the specular map. Each weapon is joined into one mesh in a canonical frame (barrel or blade along +Y in Blender, grip at the origin) and parented to a weapon root placed in front of the camera. Hands are posed by two-bone IK with a pole target, a copied hand orientation and per-finger curl angles, all described per weapon in the script's `WEAPONS` table. The clips are keyed on the weapon root (bob, sway, carry, recoil or thrust), baked to bone keys at 24 fps, pushed to NLA tracks and exported with WebP textures. `--preview DIR` renders each clip from the game camera plus close-ups of the grip for checking poses without the game.

References: [Blender glTF export](https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/dev/addons/scene_gltf2.html), [Three.js GLTFLoader](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/GLTFLoader.html), [additive animation](https://threejs.org/docs/#api/en/animation/AnimationUtils.makeClipAdditive).

### Other players

`public/models/player.glb` (1.3 MB) is the same Rocketbox soldier as a whole character: about 8,000 triangles, 54 joints, a 1K body atlas and 512 px head, helmet and equipment maps, with `idle`, `walk`, `run` and `crouch` retargeted from the Rocketbox animation library and a generated one-second `death` fall. `scripts/viewmodels/build_player.py` samples each library clip's world-space bone rotations frame by frame (the clip files carry their first frame as the bind pose, so local rotations cannot be copied directly), applies them to the avatar's own skeleton with the pelvis height, blends the last frames of each loop into the first, and places a `weapon_socket` empty on the right hand whose -Z runs along the fingers.

`PlayerModelLibrary` (`src/game/playerModel.ts`) loads the file named by `VITE_PLAYER_MODEL` once; each peer gets a `SkeletonUtils` clone that shares geometry and textures and only clones materials, so the body can carry the peer's colour tint. `RemotePlayers` keeps its primitive mannequin until the model is ready (or forever if it fails) and then drives the character from the same pose packets: idle, walk, run and crouch crossfade over 200 ms from `moving`, `running` and `crouching`; `pitch` tilts the upper spine and head about their bind-pose right axis after the mixer runs; the held procedural weapon moves onto the hand socket; health zero plays the death clip and leaves the body on the ground until respawn. Eight characters add roughly 65,000 triangles and no extra textures.

### Weapon grip and switching correction

The cyan arena-outline rendering has been removed; Rapier still enforces the playable boundary. Procedural hands now attach to the held weapon in weapon-local coordinates. Each grip is described by the gripped part's cross-section, so three-jointed fingers close around the knife handle, the pistol grip and the carbine fore-end, with the index finger on the trigger and an opposing thumb over the top. The pistol uses a supporting second hand over the strong hand; the carbine's support hand sits under the fore-end with its forearm angling down and left. Hands, wrists and forearms inherit the weapon's recoil, sway, landing and sprint motion. These procedural hands are now the fallback behind the authored models above.

Each weapon has a rest pose in `VIEW_POSES` (`src/game/weaponVisuals.ts`). Guns sit low and right of centre and yaw slightly inward so the barrel converges on the crosshair; the carbine's stock leaves the frame at the bottom right instead of filling the view. Gun bodies were slimmed so they read as firearms when seen along their side. Sprinting blends in a per-weapon carry offset that drops the weapon and cants it across the body; it settles back when the player stops running.

First-person geometry renders in a separate lit scene after clearing depth, preserving depth testing between fingers, palms and weapons without clipping against world geometry. Switching lowers the old weapon, changes it below the frame, then raises the new one over 560 ms. Local firing and leader validation both block attacks during this interval. Rapid switches retain a transition instead of snapping the visible model. Headless Chrome checks covered all three grips from the game camera and from side and top views, sprint carries, switch frames, and a scripted pickup, fire, switch and sprint sequence in the running game.
